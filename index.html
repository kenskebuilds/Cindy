Revision 4 · Fat-Loss Phase · Age 40

The Zero-Waste Protocol

A 5-day split built for a cut, with every directive traced to the literature — and an honest account of which questions turned out not to matter at all.

Mass 75 kg Body fat 20%+ Calories 2,300–2,400 Protein 180 g Deficit cap 500 kcal Warm-up 6–10 min Volume hold, don't cut
The one rule

In a cut, holding volume beats every other decision

Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer (2022, Eur J Appl Physiol) reviewed 15 studies of resistance-trained athletes in caloric restriction and found that keeping at least 10 weekly sets per muscle group — and not cutting volume as calories drop — was what tracked with little-to-no lean mass loss. Your instinct in a deficit will be to train less because you feel worse. That instinct is the thing most likely to cost you muscle.

This single variable outweighs every exercise-selection decision in this document combined. Grip width, cable versus dumbbell, which machine, what order — all of it is rounding error next to weekly sets, effort, and load held over months.

Which is worth saying plainly, because most of what you asked about turned out not to matter. The research below is organised so the things that do matter come first, and the settled questions are collapsed into a single block you can stop thinking about.

01 — Settled

Six questions you can stop asking

Each of these was tested properly and came back null. Note the pattern: the strongest study in this batch is a negative result whose authors predicted the opposite.

No difference Pulldown grip width & orientation

Buonsenso (2025), 40 trained men, seven variants — no significant difference in lat activation across any grip type, width or trunk angle. Andersen (2014) and Lusk (2010) agree. "Wide grip for lats" outlived its evidence.

No difference Iso-lateral vs bilateral

Kassiano (2025, Sports Medicine, 9-study meta-analysis): no hypertrophy difference (ES −0.21). Strength follows test specificity. Choose iso-lateral for bracing and side-to-side balance, not growth.

No difference Lateral raise: cable vs dumbbell vs machine

Larsen (2025, Front Physiol), 24 trained, within-subject, ROM-matched. The authors hypothesised cables would win and got moderate-to-extreme Bayesian support for the null. Delt thickness rose 3.3–4.6% either way.

No difference Machine vs free weight

Haugen (2023), 13 studies, n=1,016 — equal for hypertrophy, dynamic strength, isometric strength and jump. Only strength testing is modality-specific, and you have no free-weight test to be specific to.

No difference Exercise order

Nunes (2021, Eur J Sport Sci, 11-study meta-analysis): no effect of order on hypertrophy. Strength gains favour whatever sits first. And "back before chest" has no evidence base at all — it traces to gym-floor copy.

No difference Where the fly's resistance peaks

The one direct test of ascending versus descending resistance profile in a chest fly found ~18% vs ~20% pec growth, not significantly different. Selecting exercises by resistance curve is currently an aesthetic preference, not a method.

A caveat that limits all six. Most of these null findings come from untrained subjects, who grow from almost anything — which makes "no difference" weaker evidence than it looks for a trained 40-year-old. The genuinely trained-subject evidence here is about five studies deep. Treat these as "stop optimising," not as proven equivalence.
02 — Exercise selection

The three that actually change something

Day 6 does not need a biceps exercise

Literature insightTwo findings that look contradictory but aren't. Mannarino (2021, JSCR, contralateral design) found elbow flexor hypertrophy of +11.1% from a biceps curl versus +5.2% from a dumbbell row — so direct curls genuinely earn their place, and "compounds are enough for arms" is wrong. But de França (2015, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab, trained men) found that adding isolation on top of a program that already has it produces no additional size or strength. Gentil (2013) and Bezerra (2018) agree. And the study most often cited on this — Rogers (2000), showing +6.6% vs +6.5% arm circumference — is a conference abstract that never became a paper, measuring with a tape. It should carry almost no weight.

Practical applicationKeep both curls on Day 1. Add nothing to Day 6. Under fractional counting your biceps already receive 8–12 weekly sets from the pulling before any curl. In a deficit, an unspent recovery slot is not a wasted one.

The Super Incline Press is a shoulder press

Literature insightRodríguez-Ridao (2020, IJERPH, 30 trained subjects, five bench angles) found upper (clavicular) pec activation peaks at 30°, with anterior deltoid climbing steadily as the bench gets steeper and mid/lower pec output falling. Lauver (2016) agrees on 30–45°. On the hypertrophy side, Chaves (2020, 8 weeks, sets equated) found upper-pec thickness increased most in the incline-only group — though in untrained subjects at once weekly. No evidence supports 60–70° for chest. The manufacturer's own copy says the machine "closely mimics the mechanics of a front military press."

Practical applicationKeep the incline at ~30°, 45° at the outside. Above that you are buying anterior deltoid you already train and paying for it in pec stimulus. If you want a shoulder press, program a shoulder press — you already have one on Day 6.

Keep the lat pulldown — and it's the better choice for you specifically

Literature insightFirst, the honest part: no hypertrophy trial comparing pulldown to pull-up exists. Anyone calling the pulldown "one of the best vertical pulls" is extrapolating from acute EMG, which Vigotsky (2018, 2022, Sports Medicine) shows is not a validated predictor of hypertrophy. What does exist: Doma (2013, Sports Biomechanics) found no difference in latissimus activation between chin-up and pulldown — but chin-ups produced greater biceps and greater erector spinae activity. The only variant with real support to avoid is behind-the-neck (Sperandei 2009).

Practical applicationKeep it, and keep it for the right reason: it is the better option given your complaint, not a compromise. Doma's erector finding is the one result that maps directly onto your problem. This also means the Day 6 pull-up is working against you — it's been swapped for a second pulldown variation below. If pull-ups have never aggravated your back, put them back; you're the evidence that matters on that one.

Setting up the pulldown — the seven-second checklist

You asked for setup instructions rather than a single cue, and that's the right instinct: on this movement the setup is the technique. Run this before every set, not just the first.

  1. Thigh pad tight — no daylight. If your hips can lift even slightly, the load pulls you up and your erectors spend the set fighting it. This is the single biggest contributor to your soreness.
  2. Chest on the pad before rep one, and it stays there. The set ends when the chest leaves it, regardless of how many reps you had planned.
  3. Exhale fully and let the ribs drop. This kills the arch before it can start. Do it once, at the top, before you pull.
  4. Set the trunk angle once — about 10–15° back — and never change it. Every degree of rep-to-rep drift is erector work you didn't want to buy.
  5. Brace light, around 10–15%. Maximal bracing here adds spinal compression to buy stability you don't need.
  6. The mental model: drive the elbow down toward your back pocket. Not "pull the bar down" — the bar coming down is a consequence, not the intention. Think about where the elbow finishes.
  7. End the set at the rep where the torso wants to move — not the rep where the lats fail. That rep adds erector fatigue and nothing else.
Correction, from the gym floor. I read the manufacturer's page three ways and concluded the Iso-Lateral Front Lat Pulldown had only a thigh hold-down. It has a chest pad — confirmed in person, which beats a spec sheet. That upgrades the machine materially: it is now a genuinely torso-supported vertical pull, which is the exact configuration Fenwick found produced the highest lat activation and the lowest spine load simultaneously. Combined with the chest-padded Iso-Lateral Row, both of the exercises that were giving you erector fatigue now have fully supported versions available. Use the pad, don't just sit near it.

Core: one movement, loaded — not more exercises

Literature insightThe usual reassurance — "the compounds train your core" — fails on its own terms in your program. Hamlyn (2007) found no significant abdominal activation difference between 80% 1RM squats and deadlifts and bodyweight exercises; compounds are an erector stimulus, not an abdominal one. And Clark (2019) compared back squat to hack squat directly, finding the hack squat produced significantly less trunk activation across nearly every muscle and phase. Your program is deliberately built from hack squats, leg presses and machine presses — engineered to minimise trunk demand.

Practical applicationSo one direct movement is the right number, and you have it. But load it: 3 × 8–12 with a dumbbell between the feet, progressing load rather than reps. Bodyweight knee raises for 15 reps is submaximal for an intermediate lifter. Moved to Day 2 — on Day 5 it was the fifth exercise, getting whatever survived the hack squats.

The optional four minutes that genuinely earn their place. Your actual reported problem is compensatory lumbar extension under load — that's an anti-extension demand, not a flexion-strength one. Escamilla (2010) found the ab wheel rollout produces the highest abdominal and oblique activity in the literature while minimising lumbar paraspinal activation — the exact inverse of your failure mode. Two sets from the knees, partial range, at session end. Skip it if you only care about abs showing, because that is a body-fat question and nothing else: Kostek (2007, n=104, MRI) found spot reduction does not occur, and Vispute ran six weeks of seven ab exercises five days a week with no change in abdominal fat or waist girth.
And core work is not back insurance. Smith (2014, 29 trials) found strong evidence that stabilisation exercise is no better than any other active exercise long-term. Lauersen (2014) found strength training drove a ~68% injury reduction — core and proprioceptive work were not the effective ingredient. One more correction while we're here: Saraceni (2020, JOSPT) found lumbar flexion during lifting is not a risk factor for back pain, and people with back pain flexed about 6° less. Don't take a flexion phobia away from this document — the defensible claim is narrow and specific, and it's about held positions in your first waking hour.
Two things with no peer-reviewed support of any kind, despite confident marketing: the Hammer Strength converging arc (the pec's transverse-adduction function is real anatomy, but "therefore the arc grows more pec" is untested inference), and "train back before chest." Both trace to manufacturer copy and gym-floor lore. Use the machines because they're comfortable and well-built, which they are.
03 — Axial loading

Where the lumbar fatigue comes from

Literature insightFenwick, Brown & McGill (2009, JSCR) found the supported row produced the highest lat, upper back and hip extensor activation while producing the lowest lumbar erector activation and lowest spine load. The bent-over row produced the highest spine load. The standing one-arm cable row produced the greatest axial rotation — which matters because Marras (1993, Spine, >400 industrial jobs) found trunk asymmetry and velocity to be independent risk contributors with predictive power more than three times that of standard lifting guidelines.

Practical applicationChest-supported rowing is your primary horizontal pull. It won on stimulus and spine load simultaneously — not a trade.

Literature insight⚠️ Mechanistic reasoning, not measurement. No study measuring lumbar load during lat pulldown or seated cable row was found. What is documented: torso swing shifts emphasis off the lats onto the erectors, and grip variations don't meaningfully change lat activation — so the lay-back buys nothing on the prime mover.

Practical applicationYour soreness is almost certainly isometric trunk-position work. Anchor rather than deload — and with the chest pad confirmed on both the pulldown and the row, the equipment now does most of the anchoring for you. Chest on the pad, thighs under the hold-down, pelvis neutral, light brace, trunk angle never changing rep to rep. Test it in week one at the same load. If the soreness resolves, confirmed. If it doesn't, this directive is wrong and we look elsewhere — and the remaining suspect becomes the pressing, not the pulling.

Literature insightThree lines converge on morning timing. Adams, Dolan & Hutton (1987, Spine): early-morning forward bending raises bending stress ~300% in the disc (⚠️ in-vitro cadaveric creep). Tyrrell (1985, Spine), by stadiometry: 54% of the entire day's stature loss happens in the first hour after rising. And Snook (1998, Spine) — an 18-month sham-controlled RCT in 85 chronic back-pain subjects — found restricting early-morning lumbar flexion cut a 33% reduction in pain intensity (95% CI 11–55%) against a sham group given six exercises known to be ineffective.

Practical applicationMechanism, dose-time curve and a sham-controlled RCT all agree — very little in strength coaching is supported this well, and Tyrrell's 54% is why "one hour" is the right interval rather than an arbitrary one. Wake 60–90 minutes before training, not 30, and spend it upright and moving. Put any hinge at the end of the session. No static flexion stretching in the first hour — five minutes of flexion creep cuts bending resistance 42% (Adams & Dolan 1996). A morning toe-touch before hinging is exactly backwards.

Literature insightMaeo (2021, MSSE), 12-week within-subject trial: seated leg curl (hip flexed, hamstrings lengthened) produced greater hypertrophy in all three biarticular hamstrings than prone. A direct controlled test of the exact mechanism the RDL is prized for — with zero spinal flexion moment.

Practical applicationSeated leg curl is your primary hamstring builder. Keep the RDL for hip-extension strength at moderate load, 2–3 RIR, placed last in the session. That resolves the 06:30 timing conflict without losing hamstring stimulus.

An inversion worth absorbing. McGill (1991) found anterior shear rose from 492 to 736 N as load increased while shear borne by the facets and disc stayed roughly constant at ~200 N — the musculature absorbs the increment. The erectors aren't a liability to minimise; they're what keeps shear off the passive structures. The goal is selective offloading during pulling and pressing, not detraining them. Which is why cutting the 45° back extension is a volume-redundancy call, not a safety one — and why dropping it won't make you weaker.
Two cues that don't survive scrutiny. van Dieën (1999) found spinal compression not significantly different between stoop and squat lifting — "squat, don't stoop" has no load-reduction basis. Saraceni (2020, JOSPT meta-analysis) found low-quality evidence that greater lumbar flexion during lifting is not a risk factor for back pain onset. What survives: keep the load close (the only cue with unambiguous mechanical support), move slowly and symmetrically, and hold a consistent actively-controlled position.
04 — Fat loss

Cardio is your third lever, not your first

Literature insightLopez (2022, Obesity Reviews, 114 trials, 4,184 participants) found resistance training plus caloric restriction was the single most effective combination for fat loss — −3.8% body fat, −5.3 kg fat mass — ahead of every aerobic-inclusive arm, while also being best for lean mass (+0.8 kg). And Johns (2014, 8 direct comparisons, 1,022 participants) found adding exercise to a diet programme produced no significant additional weight loss at 3–6 months (−0.62 kg, CI crossing zero). Wewege (2022, 58 studies) shows resistance training independently reduces body fat percentage, fat mass and visceral fat.

Practical applicationYour five lifting sessions plus a controlled deficit are the fat-loss programme. Cardio's job is to keep the deficit small enough to protect muscle, and to buy cardiorespiratory fitness — both excellent reasons. "Burning fat" is not one of them.

Higher heart rate or Zone 2? Neither — and here's the arithmetic

Literature insightPeak fat oxidation is ~0.47 g/min at ~64% VO₂max (Achten 2002). Forty-five minutes at exactly the right intensity oxidises about 21 g of fat — roughly 190 kcal, against 7,700 kcal in a kilogram. Every outcome-first meta-analysis agrees intensity doesn't matter: Keating (2017, 31 studies, 873 participants) found no difference on any body-fat measure, and where HIIT had lower time commitment, results favoured steady-state. Sultana (2019, 47 studies) is sharpest: low-volume HIIT didn't significantly reduce fat mass at all (ES −0.129) while still improving fitness.

Practical applicationPrescribe cardio by duration, not by zone. You are buying weekly energy expenditure and aerobic base at low recovery cost. Don't raise the heart rate believing it burns more fat; don't lower it believing the same.

Two numbers that run opposite to the marketing. EPOC — the "afterburn" — is tens of kilocalories, roughly a banana, and it is the most oversold concept in commercial fitness. Meanwhile the effect nobody sells you, ~28% energy compensation (Careau 2021, n=1,754, doubly-labelled water), is about ten times larger and runs the other way: only ~72% of exercise calories show up in daily expenditure. Discount whatever your watch says by roughly a quarter before eating any of it back.

Fasted training does what it claims and none of what it promises

Literature insightFasted exercise genuinely raises fat oxidation during the session — Vieira (2016, Br J Nutr, 27 trials) confirms it robustly. It changes nothing about body composition: Schoenfeld (2014) ran the matched-hypocaloric test and found no between-group difference on any outcome. Separately, a resistance session expends roughly 200–350 kcal against 400–540 for a Zone 2 session, so lifting fasted does not substitute for cardio — the gap is in total energy, not substrate.

Practical applicationLift fasted because it suits 06:30. That's a legitimate reason. Just not the fat-loss one. Black coffee beforehand is fine and performance-motivated only — and caffeine's fat-oxidation effect is smaller in trained people (Collado-Mateo 2020).

The harder run: get the stimulus, skip the risk

Literature insightAt ~180 max, 150–165 bpm is 83–92% — threshold to VO₂max work, a two-zone jump, not a slightly harder Zone 2. Interference isn't the concern: Fyfe (2016) is titled, near enough, "endurance training intensity does not mediate interference" — the moderators are volume and frequency, not intensity. The concern is that a hard, high-impact, eccentrically-loaded run performed under-fuelled is the highest injury-risk item in your week; low energy availability affects ~49% of male athletes with consistent evidence of elevated bone stress injury risk. Against that, the case for intensity is real: Mandsager (2018, JAMA Netw Open, 122,007 patients) found fitness predicts mortality with no observed upper limit of benefit.

Practical applicationDon't turn the 5 km into a sustained hard effort while cutting. Instead, once weekly at most: 5 × 3 min at 150–165 bpm with 2–3 min easy between, after a 10-minute warm-up. Same VO₂max stimulus, a fraction of the eccentric load. Or do those intervals on the bike — near-zero eccentric load, entire cardiovascular benefit retained. Drop it in any week where sleep, recovery or joints are poor.

The lever nobody mentions. Given ~28% compensation, the most reliable exercise-side variable isn't cardio sessions at all — it's daily steps. Set a floor of 8,000–10,000/day and monitor it. The compensation literature predicts your unconscious activity will drift downward as the deficit accumulates, and falling NEAT will quietly eat your Zone 2 sessions without you noticing.
05 — Nutrition

Set the deficit, then stop fiddling

⚠️ The deficit ceiling is now properly sourced. Murphy & Koehler (2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports) meta-regression found an energy deficit impaired lean-mass gains (ES −0.57) with ~500 kcal/day the point at which lean-mass gain was abolished — strength was preserved either way. At 75 kg and 20%+ body fat, maintenance is roughly 2,550–2,700 kcal. Target 2,300–2,400 and no lower.

Aim for 0.5–0.7% of body weight per week (~0.4–0.5 kg). Garthe (2011, RCT in elite athletes) found the slower rate preserved lean mass where 1.4%/week did not. At your body fat you have room to run this 12–16 weeks.

Macros: protein 180 g (Helms 2014: 1.8–2.7 g/kg during energy restriction), fat 60–70 g, carbohydrate 240–270 g. Reassess every three weeks against the four-week rolling weight average, adjusting ±150 kcal.

Stop doing these

The second creatine doseHalve the cost

You take 10 g on lifting days. Hultman (1996, direct muscle biopsy) showed saturation is held on 2 g/day, and a 2024 dose-response meta found no dose-response above ~5 g/day. One 5 g dose, any time.

Pairing creatine with carbs on purposeLoading-phase artefact

Green (1996) and Steenge (2000) both ran 5-day loading at 20 g/day in creatine-naive men — the famous 60% figure is an advantage in filling an empty tank. Once saturated, extra insulin has nothing left to push in.

The ¼ tsp salt before liftingUnsupported

ACSM's fluid stand recommends sodium for exercise beyond one hour, and every retention study is in dehydrated or heat-stressed subjects. Keep it for the run in heat. Drink the water regardless.

CollagenI recommended this — cancelled

Shaw (2017) is n=8 measuring serum PINP, a marker predominantly reflecting bone collagen, with the tendon data from engineered ligaments in a dish. Aussieker (2023, MSSE) measured connective tissue protein synthesis directly with tracers: collagen did nothing.

Keep these

  • The 50 g shake. Trommelen (2023, Cell Reports Medicine, quadruple-tracer) fed 100 g versus 25 g and found the large bolus produced a larger and >12 h longer anabolic response. "Only 25–30 g can be used" was an artefact of studies that stopped measuring at five hours.
  • The 08:00 timing. Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger (2013) found the protein-timing effect disappeared once total protein was controlled. There is no window to miss.
  • The two dates pre-lift — but know what they do. King (2022, 21 studies): carbohydrate helps only in sessions over 45 minutes. An overnight fast changes muscle glycogen <3%. Naharudin (2020) showed a 29 kcal sham breakfast reproduced the entire benefit. It's a gastric-fill cue. Don't scale it up — and in a deficit those calories now count.
  • Four protein feedings of ~45 g, one at 21:00–22:00. Don't add a pre-sleep shake — Joy (2018), the one trial matching total protein and only moving casein to night, found no difference. Redistribute.
  • Get 25(OH)D measured before supplementing. Sist (2023, 11 RCTs) found an effect only when baseline was low. Omega-3 at 1–2 g/day is defensible on cardiovascular grounds only.
06 — Warm-up

Six minutes upper, ten minutes lower

Citation audit — one figure corrected. This section's 17 citations were re-audited after the research that produced them was interrupted. All 17 were located, but three carry caveats worth knowing. A Snook (1998) figure previously quoted here as "pain days cut 23% versus 2% in sham" was not verifiable and has been removed — the paper's confirmed result is a 33% reduction in pain intensity (95% CI 11–55%). The conclusion is unchanged; the number was wrong. Separately: Enes (2025), the most load-bearing citation here, was read as a preprint because the published version was inaccessible. Oliva (2026) is a self-described pilot whose sample size could not be obtained — it should not carry weight alone. And the RAMP protocol, widely cited in coaching, is published in a non-peer-reviewed trade magazine and is deliberately not cited as evidence anywhere in this document.

Enes (2025) compared two warm-up sets, one warm-up set, and none at all in 29 trained lifters at 10-rep loads. Differences were "negligible to small." The authors recommend skipping the specific warm-up for time efficiency.

Ribeiro (2014) found the same thing at 80% 1RM — no difference in total reps or fatigue across four warm-up conditions. So the honest starting position is that the conventional fifteen-minute ritual has no evidence demanding it, and your instinct about wasted time is closer to the data than the ritual is.

Two things stop it being zero. Abad (2011) found adding a general warm-up raised leg-press 1RM 8.4% over a specific warm-up alone, and Barroso (2013) got +3% from a low-intensity general warm-up. And separately, the morning-spine evidence — which is about risk, not performance.

Stretching — the rule is not what you were told

Literature insightThe blanket "never static stretch before lifting" rule is roughly a decade out of date. Kay & Blazevich (2012, MSSE, 106 studies) found stretches under 30 s cost 1.1% — trivial — and 30–45 s cost 1.9%, non-significant. Only 60 s or more produced meaningful decrement. Simic (2013, 104 studies) agrees the smallest effects are at ≤45 s. The impairment is real but duration-gated at about a minute per muscle, and most gym stretching is 20–30 s.

Practical applicationIf there's one stretch you've always done and it makes you feel ready, keep it — under 45 seconds, and never as your whole warm-up. But see the ban below, which is a different issue entirely.

⚠️ Banned before roughly 07:15, and this one isn't about wasted time. No held lumbar-flexion stretches in your first waking hour: toe touches, standing forward folds, seated hamstring stretches, child's pose, or any sustained rounded-back foam-rolling position. Adams & Dolan (1996) found five minutes of sustained flexion cut the spine's bending resistance by 42% across 45 cadaveric segments, and Snook's sham-controlled RCT found restricting early-morning lumbar flexion produced a 33% reduction in pain intensity. Note the mechanism is duration-dependent creep, not flexion itself — a controlled RDL is a different exposure from a 60-second hold, and rapid flexion actually increased bending resistance 10–15%. It's the held position to eliminate, not hip hinging.
The thing that matters more than any of this: if you sit hunched in a car on the way to the gym, you arrive with degraded protective reflexes — Sánchez-Zuriaga (2010) found an hour of flexed sitting raised back-muscle reflex latency from 60 ms to 96 ms, and muscle fatigue alone did not do that. Standing upright for two or three minutes before you start is higher-yield than any stretch in this section.

What to cut

  • Foam rolling. Warneke (2024, J Sport Health Sci, 38 studies, 140 effect sizes) found foam rolling and stretching produce no better range of motion or stiffness change than any other warm-up activity. The authors wrote that there "is no need to emphasize stretching or foam rolling." Five minutes gone for something a meta-analysis this size couldn't find.
  • The pre-training mobility circuit. Ankle drills, banded hip openers, 90/90s. Oliva (2026) found general mobility work reduced isometric squat force by 3.8%. And you're on a hack squat, which fixes foot position and removes the ankle constraint anyway.
  • Thoracic work before pressing. I searched specifically: no systematic review, meta-analysis or RCT testing acute thoracic mobility against overhead pressing performance or shoulder injury. Pure convention.
  • Stretching "to prevent injury." Six independent lines, tens of thousands of subjects, converging on nothing: Lauersen 2014 (RR 0.963, n=26,610), Thacker 2004 (OR 0.93), Herbert & Gabriel 2002 (BMJ), Pope 2000 (n=1,538 recruits, HR 0.95), Jamtvedt 2010 (n=2,377). This isn't an inconclusive null — it's a precisely estimated zero. In the same analysis, strength training returned RR 0.315. You're already doing the thing that works, five times a week.
  • Post-activation potentiation. Xu (2025, Sports Medicine, 62 studies) puts the effect at 0.12 — trivial — and it shrinks as training status rises. Every measured outcome is jump, sprint or change-of-direction. Not one measured hypertrophy. It also needs 4–7 minutes of rest to work at all.
  • Four or more ramping sets on anything.

Two rows, two paths — and be honest about why. This is the low row; Day 1 is the higher-elbow-path row. That pairing is not chosen because variation grows more muscle — Kassiano (2022, 8 studies) and Baz-Valle (2019, the only trained-subject RCT) both found exercise variation produces no hypertrophy benefit, only better motivation. It's chosen because a low pulling path lines up with the lat's actual mechanical action while a higher path recruits more mid-back and rear delt, so one of each covers both. That reasoning is mechanical, not evidential — the angle-and-region literature keeps returning trivial effects. Running the same machine on both days would cost you nothing measurable.
On the Day 6 → Day 1 gap. Your lat work is spaced unevenly — five days from Day 1 to Day 6, then only two back to Day 1. That's fine, because the sizes are lopsided in the helpful direction: Day 1 is the big session (6 lat sets) and gets the long gap; Day 6 is a light 3-set exposure and is what sits 48 hours before it. You go into your heaviest pulling day after three sets, not six. And distribution probably isn't a variable anyway — Schoenfeld (2019) and Pelland (2026) both found frequency has negligible hypertrophy effect once weekly volume is equated. 09 — Corrections

What earlier versions got wrong

Moving calf work away from the runReversed

Called the highest tendon risk in the schedule. No evidence exists that calf training near a run raises Achilles risk — a targeted search found nothing. Lauersen (2014, BJSM, n=26,610) found strength training cuts overuse injuries to RR 0.527, and heavy slow resistance is a Level-1 treatment for Achilles tendinopathy (Beyer 2015). The real error was the inverse: too little soleus loading, done as high-rep work. Soleus carries ~58% of triceps surae force in running (Dorn 2012) and only loads with the knee bent.

"The hip thrust spares the spine"Backwards

Otsuka (2021, PLOS ONE), trained males at 6RM: lumbosacral extension moment deadlift 410 N·m, hip thrust 385, back squat 316. The hip thrust runs ~24% above the squat. Keep it for glutes; stop calling it offloading.

"The bench arch is a lumbar risk"Not evidenced

No study measuring lumbar load during bench press exists, and no injury epidemiology attributes lumbar injury to the arch. The erector fatigue is still worth eliminating — recovery spent on a non-target muscle. The injury claim is withdrawn.

Dead hangs for decompressionWithdrawn

Wegner (2013, Cochrane, 32 RCTs, 2,762 participants): traction makes little or no difference to pain, function or return to work. Hang for grip and shoulders if you enjoy it. The intervention that restores disc height is lying down.

The 10% run rule & the 180 spm cadence targetBoth folklore

Buist (2008, RCT, 532 runners): ~10%/week graded versus standard gave 20.8% vs 20.3% injury incidence. Revised ceiling is ~30% per fortnight (Nielsen 2014). And Szymanek (2020, 381 runners, prospective): 172 spm uninjured vs 173 injured. You don't need to measure cadence, because you shouldn't be chasing it.

"Frequency drives hypertrophy"Right call, wrong reason

Schoenfeld (2019, 25 studies) and Pelland (2026, 67 studies / 2,058 participants) both find frequency has negligible effect on hypertrophy once weekly volume is equated. Frequency is a strength variable. The real reason to split legs is the per-session ceiling, past which late sets run at degraded output.

Collagen, and "180 g protein is just insurance"Both reversed

Collagen: Kirmse (2024) meta-analysed 13 trials — strength SMD 0.079, p = 0.445, 48 of 55 parameters null, 11 of 13 studies industry-linked. Cancelled. Protein: 1.6 g/kg is the plateau in a surplus, but in a deficit protein does muscle-retention work — Helms (2014) puts the range at 1.8–2.7 g/kg. Your original 180 g was right.

Two citations I got wrong myselfOwn error

I briefed the research with two claims that don't hold. Andersen (2014) is a grip-width study within the pulldown, not a pulldown-versus-pull-up comparison. And there is no Schoenfeld chest fly versus bench press RCT — the paper I was reaching for is an unreviewed preprint by different authors comparing two profiles within the fly, finding no difference. Neither changed a recommendation, but the record should show it.

11 — Verification

How to know it worked

  1. The postural test — week one. Lock the trunk on the pulldown and row at the same load. If erector soreness resolves, the mechanism is confirmed and your load never had to drop. If it doesn't, that directive is wrong. Cheapest test in the program.
  2. The morning-hinge test — four weeks. Hinge out of the first waking hour and placed last. Snook's RCT predicts symptom reduction; if none appears, the diurnal mechanism isn't your driver.
  3. Strength retention is the real endpoint in a cut. Holding load at matched RIR is success. If your indicator lifts hold for 12 weeks while weight falls 0.5% per week, the program worked — that is the outcome to judge it on, not the mirror.
  4. Weight trend. Four times weekly, fasted, four-week rolling average. 0.5–0.7% per week. Faster costs lean mass.
  5. Steps. Track them like a training variable, because they are one.
  6. Performance as the only fatigue marker. Two consecutive weeks of unexplained decline at matched RIR is your deload trigger — one week at 50% sets, load held constant. Not soreness, not HRV, not motivation.
  7. Tendon check. Morning Achilles and patellar stiffness on run day +1. Anything past 24 hours, hold running volume. Never run through Achilles symptoms — prior tendinopathy carries OR 6.3, dwarfing every training-load variable.
General training and nutrition analysis, not medical advice. Persistent or sharp lumbar pain, and any tendon symptom outlasting 24 hours, warrants assessment by a physiotherapist or physician before continuing to load it. Citations were verified by literature search; where a study's numbers could not be retrieved from full text, where a claim rests on mechanistic reasoning rather than measurement, or where the underlying subjects were untrained, that is marked in the text rather than smoothed over.
