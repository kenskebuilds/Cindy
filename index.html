The Deficit Hypertrophy Protocol

The one rule

The deficit is the only thing that should get smaller

Murphy & Koehler (2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports, meta-analysis and meta-regression of RCTs ≥3 weeks) found that training in an energy deficit impairs gains in lean mass but not strength, and that the regression crossed zero at roughly 500 kcal·day⁻¹ — beyond that deficit, lean mass gains stopped. Independently, Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer (2022, Eur J Appl Physiol, 15 studies of resistance-trained athletes in caloric restriction) found that holding at least 10 weekly sets per muscle and not cutting volume as calories drop was what tracked with little-to-no lean mass loss.

Two numbers govern this entire document: the deficit stays at or under 500 kcal, and the weekly set count never gets cut because you feel flat. Everything else on this page is downstream of those.

This matters because the instinct in a cut runs the other way. Training feels worse, sessions feel longer, and the natural response is to trim sets and add cardio. Both meta-analyses above say that is the exact inversion of what works: the deficit is the variable to keep small, and the volume is the variable to defend.

The second constraint shaping every page here is the lumbar spine. It is not treated as a mobility problem to be stretched away — it is treated as a loading budget. Axial load is spent deliberately on the two or three lifts that genuinely need it, and every other slot in the week is filled with a supported or machine-based alternative that buys the same hypertrophy at a lower spinal cost.

What changed from the previous revision, and why. The prior plan was audited against nine constraints and failed three of them outright. It had no periodisation at all — no accumulation, no deload. It had no stated progression rule — rep ranges and RIR but no trigger for adding load or sets. And seven muscles were trained once a week: chest, glutes, biceps, front delts, rear delts, abs, and each calf head individually. Weekly volume has barely moved. What moved is how it is distributed, and what tells you when to change it. The full audit is in the Validation tab.
01 — Warm-up

Six minutes upper, nine minutes lower, and nothing else

The evidence base for the conventional fifteen-minute ritual is thin to the point of embarrassment. Enes (2025, 29 trained lifters) compared two warm-up sets, one warm-up set, and none at all at 10-rep loads and found differences "negligible to small," recommending the specific warm-up be skipped for time efficiency. Ribeiro (2014) found the same at 80% 1RM — no difference in total reps or fatigue across four warm-up conditions. ⚠️ Enes was read as a preprint; the published version was not accessible. Treat as medium confidence.

Two things stop the answer being zero. Abad (2011) found adding a general warm-up raised leg-press 1RM 8.4% over a specific warm-up alone, and Barroso (2013) got +3% from a low-intensity general warm-up — and, importantly, found that long moderate-intensity warm-ups cost 4% of 1RM. Easy means easy. The second reason is spinal, and it is about risk rather than performance; it is why the lower-day warm-up is three minutes longer than the upper one. This matters here specifically because you train within about an hour of waking — the whole morning-spine section below is load-bearing rather than precautionary, and it is also why the RDL sits fourth on Day 2 rather than first.

No held lumbar-flexion stretches in your first waking hour: toe touches, standing forward folds, seated hamstring stretches, child's pose, or any sustained rounded-back foam-rolling position. Adams & Dolan (1996) found five minutes of sustained flexion cut the spine's bending resistance by 42% across 45 cadaveric segments, and Snook's sham-controlled RCT (1998) found restricting early-morning lumbar flexion produced a 33% reduction in pain intensity (95% CI 11–55%). The mechanism is duration-dependent creep, not flexion itself — a controlled RDL is a different exposure from a 60-second hold, and rapid flexion actually increased bending resistance 10–15%. It is the held position to eliminate, not hip hinging.
Higher-yield than any stretch in this section: if you sit hunched in a car on the way to the gym, you arrive with degraded protective reflexes. Sánchez-Zuriaga (2010) found an hour of flexed sitting raised back-muscle reflex latency from 60 ms to 96 ms, and muscle fatigue alone did not do that. Stand upright for two or three minutes before you start.
02 — The program

Five lifting days, two cardio days

Order within a session is by priority: whatever you most want to progress goes first, while fresh. Not because it grows more — Nunes (2021, Eur J Sport Sci, 11-study meta-analysis) found no effect of order on hypertrophy — but because it accumulates more load over months.

How every session is ordered — the rule, and what it rests on

Sequencing in earlier drafts was inherited rather than reasoned, and it showed: calves sat in the middle of Day 2, and Day 5 ran a leg extension before a lunge. Both days have been rebuilt against an explicit rule, applied in this order:

  1. The heavy tier goes first. Nunes (2021, Eur J Sport Sci, 11 studies) found the exercise placed first accumulates the most strength — hypertrophy is unaffected by order, so first position is spent on whatever you most want to get stronger at.
  2. The day’s priority lift goes first even when it is single-joint. Same meta-analysis: single-joint exercises gain nearly twice as much from first position as multi-joint ones. This is why Day 2 opens with a leg curl.
  3. Multi-joint before single-joint — but only within a shared muscle. Pre-exhaustion costs 10–30% load on the following compound with no hypertrophy return (Fisher 2014; Nunes 2021). That penalty only exists if the two movements share tissue, so a leg extension can sit ahead of an RDL and cost nothing.
  4. Calves and trunk go last, always. They interfere with nothing and nothing interferes with them, so they are the cheapest thing to displace when the clock runs out.
  5. Within the isolation block, the arm exercise goes last — not first. This looks like it violates rule 2, and it is the one place where a competing principle wins. On the two upper days the elbow flexors or extensors are already pre-fatigued by seven compound sets before any isolation starts, so moving the curl or the extension earlier does not find them fresh. What it would do is remove the ten minutes of unrelated delt work that currently sits between the compounds and the arm work — and that gap functions as an extended rest interval, which Grgic (2017) and Singer et al. (2024) find is at worst neutral and possibly favourable for trained lifters. So the arm work goes last to buy recovery, not because it matters least.
  6. One documented exception: the RDL is placed fourth on Day 2 despite being the day’s heaviest hinge. The morning lumbar-creep evidence and the deliberate load reduction from pre-fatigued hamstrings both outrank the ordering evidence there — reasoning in the Day 2 table.

On the two lower days mixing anterior and posterior work — this is deliberate. Day 2 is posterior-led and Day 5 is quad-led: the naming describes which muscle owns the heavy tier and the first three slots, not an exclusive territory. Each day then carries two or three cheap machine sets for the other half, placed last. The reason is arithmetic, not preference — with only two lower sessions, a strict anterior/posterior split forces every lower-body muscle to one exposure a week, which is exactly the failure this revision exists to fix. Quads get 2 sets on Day 2 and hamstrings get 3 on Day 5 for that reason alone, and both are leg-machine isolation with no bearing on the session's main work. (Earlier drafts named these days "Posterior + Core" and "Anterior," which implied an exclusivity the plan never had. The names are now accurate; the structure has not changed.)

Rest periods: heavy compounds 2.5–3 min, moderate compounds 2–2.5 min, isolation 60–90 s. Grgic (2017, Eur J Sport Sci) concluded that longer inter-set rest may be advantageous specifically for trained lifters, and the Bayesian re-analysis in Singer et al. (2024, Front Sports Act Living) found the evidence favours ≥60 s but is genuinely equivocal above that. The prescription above is the conservative reading: buy full rest where the set is heavy and the cost is small, spend it where the set is light and the clock matters.

Why the seated calf raise was cut, and why two calf exercises on one day was the wrong design. The plan ran seated and standing raises on both lower days, on the standard reasoning that the soleus only loads with the knee bent and therefore needs its own exercise. That reasoning is mechanically true and turns out not to matter.

Kinoshita et al. (2023, Frontiers in Physiology) trained 14 adults for 12 weeks in a within-subject unilateral model, one leg standing and one leg seated, 5 × 10 at 70% 1RM twice weekly. Standing grew the lateral gastrocnemius +12.4% vs +1.7% and the medial +9.2% vs +0.6% (both p ≤ 0.002, large effects). Soleus grew 2.1% standing vs 2.9% seated — trivial and non-significant (d = 0.2). The authors concluded standing was "by far more effective," and attributed it to training the gastrocnemius at long muscle length. ⚠️ 14 untrained participants, no control group. Within-subject design makes the comparison strong; the untrained sample makes the transfer to you weaker. Medium confidence.

So the seated raise was not "too much" — it was buying one muscle that the standing raise already grows just as well, at the price of an extra exercise on both lower days. Cut. Calves go from 8 sets across two exercises to 6 sets across one, and both lower sessions lose about four minutes. If calf growth stalls for a full block at matched loads, adding seated work back is the first thing to try — the evidence against it is one small study, not a settled question.
Why glutes were raised from 7 sets to 10 — a correction, and the reasoning that forced it. The first version of this document capped glutes below band and justified it as "the lumbar constraint being paid for." That conflated two different things: glute training and axially expensive glute training. The constraint was never a reason to hold glute volume down — only a reason to buy it with movements that do not load the spine.

And the direction of the evidence runs the other way. Gluteus medius weakness and hip extensor weakness are consistently more prevalent in people with chronic low back pain (Cooper et al. 2016, Eur Spine J, cross-sectional; Sadler et al. 2019, systematic review), and the gluteus maximus is a primary synergist for lumbar extension — so a weak one shifts that work onto the erectors. The intervention evidence is weaker but points the same way: a meta-analysis of 8 RCTs, n=508 found hip strengthening reduced pain (MD −0.66, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.48) and disability (SMD −0.81, 95% CI −1.53 to −0.10) in the short term. ⚠️ Graded "very low certainty" by its own authors — high risk of bias, no medium- or long-term effect, and at least one RCT found no benefit from adding hip strengthening to standard care. Causation is also unresolved: pain may inhibit the glutes rather than weak glutes causing pain.

What changed, and what did not. The hip thrust stays capped at 4 sets — Otsuka's ~24%-above-a-back-squat lumbosacral moment is still real. The extra volume is a fifth hip thrust set and a hip-dominant Smith lunge (Day 5, counted as 3 glute sets), which load the glute at two different hip positions.

A B-stance hip thrust sat here for one revision and has been removed. It was placed immediately after the bilateral hip thrust on the same day, doing the same joint action on the same muscle, justified only by a mechanical inference that halving the load per side would lower the peak lumbosacral moment. That inference has no data behind it, and the row was exactly the kind of unjustified variation this document bans elsewhere — constraint E, applied to itself. Net: glutes 7 → 10 sets, axial load unchanged.

Lower-body priority: plantarflexors first, quads second, hip extensors third — and one stated reason that the evidence refutes. Upper-body priority is straightforward: back, chest and shoulders, because that is the physique goal. Lower body was set against three stated aims — running, long-term health, and "supporting upper-body gains." The evidence separates those cleanly.

Plantarflexors, soleus especially. The soleus is the largest single contributor to vertical support and forward propulsion in running (Hamner & Delp 2013), triceps surae is roughly 25% of running's metabolic cost, and 14 weeks of plantarflexor training raised Achilles stiffness 31% and cut metabolic cost 4% (Albracht & Arampatzis 2013, n=13–14). In the closest study to your demographic — Eihara et al. (2024, Eur J Sport Sci), RCT, 20 recreational runners aged 41–44 — change in calf raise 1RM was the only strength change that correlated with improved oxygen cost (r = −0.477, p = 0.046); leg press and leg curl did not. ⚠️ Small n, borderline and post-hoc. Medium confidence. Soleus also loads only with the knee bent, which is why the seated raise came back.

Quadriceps. The strongest longevity case of any lower-body measure: in the Health ABC cohort (Newman et al. 2006, n≈2,292) quadriceps strength predicted mortality while leg muscle mass did not, and the association survived adjustment for muscle size. NHANES analyses put each 15 N of knee-extensor strength at 7% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.93, CI 0.91–0.95). Quadriceps is also the fastest-atrophying lower-limb group with age, driven by type II fibre loss (Nilwik 2013; Mitchell 2012). ⚠️ All observational, mostly in adults 65+. A prognostic marker, not proven causation, and you are 40. The corollary matters: train legs for strength expression, not just size. The mass-focused lens that suits your upper body is the wrong one here.

Hip extensors, third and hedged. Honestly the thinnest of the three — see the injury note below.
⚠️ Three things the evidence contradicts, stated plainly because they were part of the brief.

1. "A strong lower body supports upper-body muscle gains." This is refuted, not merely unproven. The mechanism behind it is the acute-hormone hypothesis, and it has been directly tested. West et al. (2010, J Appl Physiol) ran 15 weeks of unilateral elbow-flexor training with and without a prior high-volume leg bout and found no difference in hypertrophy or strength. West et al. (2009, J Physiol), Mitchell et al. (2013, PLoS ONE) and Morton et al. (2016) agree; Phillips (2012) titled his commentary "chasing a hormonal ghost." The single supporting study (Rønnestad 2011) had n=9, showed the effect at one of several measurement sites, was publicly questioned, and has not been replicated. Since recovery capacity is finite and in a deficit it is reduced, excess leg volume plausibly costs you upper-body progress. Train legs on their own merits — running, healthspan, function — not as an upper-body accessory.

2. Lifting will not raise your VO₂max. The running-economy meta-analysis (Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024, Sports Medicine, 31 trials, 652 runners) found high-load strength training improved economy (ES −0.266) and time-trial performance (ES −0.469) while VO₂max, vVO₂max and lactate threshold were all unchanged (p > 0.23). The lifting works on the economy and force-production axis; the running does the aerobic work. Both are worth doing, for different reasons.

3. Strength training is not reliable injury-proofing for running. Peterson et al. (2022, Sports Med Open), 30 prospective studies, 3,404 non-elite runners, tested over 100 candidate risk factors. The only significant strength finding was knee-extension strength at SMD −0.19 — which the authors themselves call trivial and possibly not clinically important. Plantarflexor, hamstring and hip-abductor strength: all null. Hip strength and patellofemoral pain is null prospectively even though it looks strong cross-sectionally (Rathleff 2014) — the weakness follows the pain rather than causing it. The best-supported modifiable risk factor in running injury is training-load management and prior injury history, not muscle strength. Lift for economy and healthspan; do not sell it to yourself as injury insurance.
Why total sets per session is the wrong thing to balance — and what to balance instead. Before the rebalance the week ran 14 / 22 / 19 / 21 / 24, which looks badly lopsided. It mostly was not, because fatigue accumulates per muscle, not per session. Day 6's 24 sets were spread across seven muscles at 3–4 sets each; Day 1's 14 sets sat on only three muscles. By the metric that matters, the light day was the more concentrated one.

Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos (2025) meta-regressed per-session volume and put the point of undetectable outcome superiority at roughly 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy — beyond that, returns flatten. ⚠️ SportRxiv preprint, not peer-reviewed, and the authors note there is little data at very high per-session volumes. Medium confidence. Against that number, the most concentrated slot in this program is chest on Day 3 at 9 direct sets — the only place in the week approaching the ceiling. Nothing else exceeds 7. It was left at 9 deliberately: moving a set to Day 6 would take the pec deck to a single token set, and 9 is still comfortably under 11 on a figure that comes from a preprint. The trigger to act on, stated concretely: if your last pec deck set declines at matched load for two consecutive weeks while the chest press is still progressing, move one set from Day 3 to Day 6 — 8 / 4 instead of 9 / 3. That is a per-session ceiling problem, not a weekly volume problem, and adding sets would make it worse.

So the rebalance was done for the clock and for local fatigue, not because the old distribution was costing growth. Three side delt sets moved to Day 1: the week is now 17–22 sets and 51–71 minutes door-to-door, instead of 14–24 sets and 54–79 minutes. Cutting the seated calf raise took Day 2 down further, to 20 sets and about 56 minutes.

Loading tiers, the four-week block, when to change what

04 — Loading

Three tiers, and the reason for each is fatigue, not growth

Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger (2017, JSCR, 21 studies) compared ≤60% 1RM against >60% 1RM with all sets taken to momentary failure and found hypertrophy was equivalent across the range, while 1RM strength gains significantly favoured heavy loads. Lasevicius (2018, Eur J Sport Sci, within-subject, volume-equated) found 40%, 60% and 80% 1RM produced similar hypertrophy while 20% did not — placing the lower boundary at roughly 30–40%, not lower.

If load does not determine hypertrophy, then load should be chosen for what it does determine: joint stress, spinal exposure, and how much of the session's fatigue budget the set spends.

That is the entire logic of the three tiers below. Heavy work is confined to the one supported machine at the front of each session, where the load buys strength and tendon adaptation and the support removes the axial penalty that normally makes heavy work expensive for a forty-year-old lumbar spine. Light work carries the lengthened-position stimulus and the metabolite-heavy finishing sets, where the load is trivially low and the set can be taken to genuine failure at almost no systemic cost.

Heavy ~75–85% 1RM · 6–8 reps · 2 RIR · rest 2.5–3 min

Exactly one exercise per lifting day, always first, always supported: the pulldown (D1), the machine chest press (D3), the hack squat (D5). Day 2's leg curl and Day 6 carry no heavy tier at all. This is where strength gain lives — Schoenfeld 2017 found heavy load matters for 1RM even where it does not for size.

Moderate ~65–75% 1RM · 8–12 reps · 1–2 RIR · rest 2–2.5 min

The second and third compounds: rows, incline press, hip thrust, shoulder press, Smith lunge, RDL. The workhorse tier — enough load to progress on, light enough that three or four sets do not blow the session's recovery budget.

Light ~40–60% 1RM · 12–20 reps · 0–1 RIR · rest 60–90 s

All isolation, all lengthened-position work, all cable and machine single-joint movements. Every set taken to failure in the program lives in this tier. It is where volume is cheap — and in a deficit, cheap volume is the only kind worth adding.

Two exceptions, both deliberate. The calf raises run at 6–8 reps with a three-second eccentric — heavier and slower than their isolation status would suggest, because tendon adaptation is driven by loading magnitude rather than rep count (Bohm). The Romanian deadlift runs at 6–8 reps but at 2–3 RIR rather than 2 — it is the only barbell lift in the program and the only one where a missed rep is a spinal event rather than a training one.
05 — The block

Three weeks flat, one week down

Volume is constant for three weeks, then halved for one. Nothing is added mid-block. The only thing that changes week to week is load — which is what makes a stalled lift mean something.

On "priority muscles," stated honestly. Allocating more sets to some muscles than others is a goal-driven choice, not an evidence-based one. No study has compared a volume-prioritised programme against an evenly-distributed one. No meta-analysis has established muscle-specific volume landmarks — Pelland et al. (2025, 67 studies, n=2,058) classifies sets as direct or indirect relative to the measured muscle and does not stratify by muscle group at all, and the ACSM 2026 position stand gives a single generic ~10 sets per muscle per week. Published MEV/MAV/MRV-per-muscle tables are practitioner heuristics with no peer-reviewed validation.

What is evidence-based is the mechanism the choice runs on: a volume dose–response with diminishing returns that appears to operate similarly across muscles. Directing more sets at a muscle is therefore a reasonable way to bias growth toward it — but which muscles is a preference, and it should be the trainee’s preference rather than the author’s. Here it is: back, chest and shoulders, stated by the trainee as the physique goal. Quads are the one addition made on evidence rather than preference — the knee-extensor strength/mortality association and the age-related type II atrophy data, both set out in Weekly totals. Calves are prioritised too, but through load rather than sets, because the running-economy case is about tendon stiffness and strength, not size. And note what "priority" now means in practice, since volume is flat: no muscle gets extra sets at any point in the block. Priority shows up as first position in the session, ownership of the heavy tier, and being the last thing cut when the clock runs out — all of which are free, unlike sets. One caution worth recording: the only muscle-specific volume signal in the literature, a subgroup finding in Baz-Valle (2022), favoured triceps at 20+ sets while quadriceps showed no such benefit — which is close to the opposite of the usual priority list. It is underpowered and is a difference in significance rather than a tested interaction, so it settles nothing. It is here because it is the only muscle-specific evidence that exists.

Week 1 Calibrate — at maintenance

98 sets. Maintenance calories, not deficit. Establish honest loads across every exercise. If in doubt, go lighter — you are calibrating, not testing.

Week 2 Same sets, deficit starts

98 sets, unchanged. Drop to a ≤500 kcal deficit. Progress load only — double progression on every lift that earned it.

Week 3 Same sets again

98 sets, unchanged. Load progression continues. This is the hardest week of the block; expect the logbook to move slower than week 2.

Week 4 Deload

Sets halved (~49). Load unchanged. Every set at 4–5 RIR. Nothing to failure. Cardio unchanged. Skip the Day 7 intervals; run easy or bike.

Confidence: low-to-medium, and here is the conflict. Deloading is near-universal in practice and thinly evidenced in the literature. Coleman et al. (2024, PeerJ, n=50, 9-week supervised RT) inserted a one-week cessation at the midpoint and found no difference in hypertrophy but worse lower-body strength in the deload group, plus slightly better readiness-to-train scores in the continuous group. Taken at face value, that argues against deloading. Three things stop it settling the question: it tested complete cessation rather than a reduced-volume week; the subjects were not in an energy deficit; and no trial has tested deloading in the population this document is written for. Per the conservative-option rule, the plan keeps a reduced-volume deload — which is not what Coleman tested — and keeps load unchanged, which is the specific variable Coleman found cessation cost. If you would rather follow Coleman directly, run four continuous weeks and take the deload only when performance actually declines. Both are defensible. This document locks the scheduled version because a 500 kcal deficit sustained over months is exactly the context where accumulated fatigue is most likely to outrun your ability to notice it.
⚠️ Volume is flat inside the block, and that is a change from my own first draft. Earlier versions added 4 sets in week 2 and 4 more in week 3. That is the conventional design, and the trial closest to your situation does not support it.

Enes et al. (2024, J Appl Physiol) randomised resistance-trained men to maintain their prior weekly volume or increase it by 30% or 60%, lower body, 8 weeks. The maintenance group gained the most: anterior thigh thickness +1.07 cm, versus +0.76 cm at +30% and +0.70 cm at +60%. Maximal strength also favoured maintenance. ⚠️ Only 29 of 55 randomised completed (~47% dropout), 9–10 per group, 8 weeks, lower body only — and a null result is not proof of harm.

Against it: Schoenfeld et al. (2019) found hypertrophy increased across 1, 3 and 5 sets per exercise in 34 trained men, and Pelland et al. (2025) put the posterior probability of a positive volume–hypertrophy slope at 100%. The reconciliation is probably that the dose–response plateaus, and where you already sit on the curve decides whether added sets do anything. At 98 weekly sets with every priority muscle in the 10–13 range, you are not on the steep part of that curve.

Three reasons flat wins here, in order of how well they are evidenced. First, Enes is the only trial testing this design in trained men and it favours maintenance. Second, a 500 kcal deficit reduces recovery capacity, which shifts the cost–benefit of extra volume against you — reasoning, not a tested finding. Third, holding volume constant means load is the only thing changing, so a stalled lift is interpretable.

One honest correction to the reasoning behind this choice. Flat volume was chosen partly as injury prevention, and that specific rationale is not evidenced — no study shows that adding four weekly sets raises injury risk in resistance training, and the running-injury literature found training-load management and prior injury history, not volume as such, to be the levers that matter (Peterson 2022). The decision is sound; the injury argument is the weakest of the reasons for it. Confidence in flat volume: medium. Confidence that accumulation would have been better: low.

If you ever want to test accumulation: add one set each to back, chest, side delts and quads in weeks 2 and 3 only, at the end of a session, light tier. If any lift on those muscles fails to match the previous week’s reps at the same load, that muscle drops straight back to week 1 volume. Twice for the same muscle means it does not tolerate added volume in a deficit.
Blocks after the first. Run four blocks (16 weeks) before changing exercises. Week 1 of each new block starts from the loads you finished the previous week 3 with. Sets never change — 98 every training week, halved only in deload. Load is the only variable that ratchets, which is what makes a stall interpretable.
06 — Progression

Double progression, and three rules for what to change

The rule, in one line: when you hit the top of the prescribed rep range on every working set at the target RIR, add the smallest available load increment next session and rebuild through the range from the bottom.

Worked example. Day 3 machine chest press is 4 × 6–8 at 2 RIR. You log 8, 8, 8, 8 at 2 RIR. Next session: add one pin or the smallest plate, expect 6, 6, 6, 6, and work back up over the following two or three sessions. You log 8, 8, 7, 6 instead? Stay at that load. The set count and the RIR never move to make the load move — that is what separates this from just grinding.

ChangeTriggerHow muchWhy this trigger
Add load Top of the rep range on every working set, at the target RIR. Smallest increment available. Weekly if earned. The default and most frequent change. Load progression at fixed sets and fixed RIR is the only unambiguous evidence that the stimulus is still growing.
Add sets Not inside a block. Only between blocks, and only if a muscle has stalled on load for two consecutive blocks while recovery is otherwise fine. +1 set, light tier, end of the session. Volume drives hypertrophy (Pelland 2025, 100% posterior probability) — but the one trial testing added volume in trained men found maintenance outperformed both +30% and +60% (Enes 2024). Sets are the last lever, not the first, and never the reactive one. See The block.
Change the exercise Two consecutive sessions of declined performance at matched effort, or pain. Swap for the listed substitute — see Practical notes. Variation is not a hypertrophy driver: Kassiano (2022, 8 studies) and Baz-Valle (2019, the only trained-subject RCT) both found exercise variation produces no hypertrophy benefit, only better motivation. So a swap must be justified by a failing lift, never by boredom or novelty.
Cut volume Session runs past 75 min, or performance falls two weeks running across multiple lifts. Drop sets in the documented order — see below. The correct response to too much work is less work. It is never more exercises, shorter rests, or supersets — all three trade measurable stimulus for a shorter clock.
Effort, defined precisely, because "close to failure" is where most programs get vague. Refalo (2023, Sports Medicine, systematic review with meta-analysis) found a small hypertrophy advantage for sets closer to failure, and the follow-up meta-regression (Refalo 2024, Sports Medicine) found the dose–response curve flattens as you approach failure — most of the benefit is captured by about 2 RIR, with little left above it. That is why this program puts almost everything at 0–2 RIR and takes only the cheap sets to zero. In this plan, true momentary failure is reached on exactly one set per exercise, on the final set of light-tier single-joint work only. Everything heavy, everything compound, everything axially loaded stops with reps in reserve — and the RDL stops with two or three.
The weekly landmine. If a session is running long or you are under-recovered, cut in this order and stop as soon as you are back under the cap: (1) Day 5 Smith lunge down to 2 sets, (2) Day 5 hip abduction down to 2 sets, (3) the third set of any light-tier isolation, (4) Day 1 face pull down to 2 sets. Calves and trunk are already last in every session, so if you simply run out of time they are what you lose — that is the design, not an accident. Do not touch the heavy tier, and do not touch anything that would take a muscle below two weekly exposures.

The three placement rules, and what backs each

1 — Cardio never precedes lifting. Not on any day, not in any version of the week.

The evidence behind this

Schumann (2022, Sports Medicine, 43 studies) found that sequence did not meaningfully affect hypertrophy or maximal strength — regardless of modality, frequency, training status, age above or below 40, or whether the sessions were same-session, same-day or separate days. But the same analysis found explosive strength gains reduced by roughly 28% when the two were combined, most strongly within a single session. Since the cost of putting cardio second is zero and the cost of putting it first is a possible decrement in the session that is doing the actual muscle-building, the rule is free. Confidence: high for "it does not matter much," high for "second is never worse."

2 — Cycling and incline walking are the default; running is confined to one session and can be dropped entirely.

The evidence behind this — and a genuine conflict

This is the one place in the document where two good sources disagree. Wilson (2012, JSCR, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes) found that resistance training performed concurrently with running, but not cycling, produced significant decrements in both hypertrophy and strength — attributed to running's greater eccentric load and muscle damage. Schumann (2022), a decade later and with more studies, found no modality effect at all.

Neither is dismissible. Wilson is older and its running studies skew toward higher volumes than prescribed here; Schumann is larger and more recent but pools across a wider range of designs. The choice locked here: default to cycling and incline walking. If Schumann is right, that choice costs nothing — the modality is irrelevant and you have lost only running specificity. If Wilson is right, it protects the primary outcome. There is no scenario where preferring the bike is the wrong bet, which is what makes it the conservative option. Running survives in exactly one weekly slot, and only because of what it offers that cycling cannot: weight-bearing bone and tendon loading, which matters more at 40 than at 25. Confidence: medium — the modality question is genuinely unsettled.

3 — Zone 2, not intervals, for almost all of it. One optional interval session a week, maximum, and none in the deload week.

The evidence behind this

Two independent reasons, neither of which is "Zone 2 burns more fat" — it does not, and the fat-loss work is done by the deficit. First, interference in the Wilson analysis correlated with the frequency and duration of endurance work, and high-intensity work costs disproportionately more recovery per minute. Second, and more practically: cardiorespiratory fitness predicts mortality with no observed upper limit (Mandsager, 122,007 patients) — so there is a real reason to keep some high-intensity work in the week, but no reason to buy it at the price of the lifting. One session is the compromise. Confidence: medium-high.

Why the total is capped where it is. About 28% of exercise-induced energy expenditure disappears to compensation (Careau) — mostly through NEAT falling. Adding cardio past this point produces less deficit than the arithmetic suggests while spending real recovery. If fat loss stalls, the correct lever is the diet or the step count, not another cardio session.

The time-crunched variant — same six days, ~40 minutes each

Built by cutting sets, never by cutting exercises or shortening rests. Every muscle keeps both exposures; the week drops from 97 sets to roughly 70, which is still above the 10-set deficit floor on every priority muscle.

  • Heavy tier drops from 4 sets to 3. Applies to the Day 1 pulldown, Day 3 chest press and Day 5 hack squat. Rest stays at 2.5–3 min — this is the one place not to economise.
  • Every light-tier isolation drops to 2 sets. The last set was already the one taken to failure, so the failure set survives.
  • Take the Day 5 Smith lunge to 2 sets and hip abduction to 1. Glute-max volume falls to roughly 6 sets and gluteus medius to 1, which is the short version’s one knowing compromise.
  • Cardio moves off the lifting days. Days 1 and 3 become lifting only; Day 4 and Day 7 absorb the volume. You lose ~50 min/week of Zone 2, so hold the step floor harder.
  • Do not superset, and do not cut rest below 60 s. Both trade measurable stimulus for a shorter clock, which is the opposite of the trade you are trying to make.

What to drop first, in order

This ordering is the same one used by the weekly landmine in Progression, extended down to the point where you should stop and reconsider the deficit instead.

  1. Day 5 Smith lunge, down to 2 sets — highest bracing tax in the session. Do not zero it; it is one of only two glute-max exposures in the week.
  2. Day 5 hip abduction, down to 2 sets — cheap, but do not zero it: it is one of the two glute exposures.
  3. Third set of any light-tier isolation — take them to two, starting with the exercises whose muscle has three weekly exposures.
  4. Day 1 face pull, down to 2 sets — rear delts still receive substantial fractional volume from 13 pulling sets.
  5. Day 7 interval session — the highest injury-risk item in the week when you are under-fuelled.
  6. Day 4 cardio session — but keep the step floor. The steps are worth more than the walk.
  7. Stop here and raise calories instead. If you are still under-recovered after all six, the problem is the deficit, not the program — and Murphy & Koehler says the deficit is the variable that was supposed to stay small.
⚠️ You train alone, so three lifts have a modified failure rule. The failure rule elsewhere in this document — final set of light-tier single-joint work goes to momentary failure — assumes you can fail a rep safely. Three lifts do not qualify, and none of them was a failure lift anyway; this makes it explicit rather than implied.

Flat dumbbell bench press (Day 6) — stop at 1–2 RIR, always. A failed rep here means dropping dumbbells from a supine position with no one to take them. If you cannot set the dumbbells down under control at the end of a set, the load is too heavy regardless of how the reps felt. Incline dumbbell press (Day 3) — same rule, same reason. Romanian deadlift (Day 2) — already capped at 2–3 RIR and flagged never-to-zero; training alone is a second reason, not a new one.

Everything taken to failure in this plan is a machine or a cable: pec deck, lateral raises, pushdowns, curls, leg extension, leg curl, face pull, standing calf raise. All of them can be failed safely with the weight going nowhere dangerous. That is not a coincidence — it is why the failure sets were put where they were.
What is explicitly not in this plan, and will not be added. No second chest press, no third row variation, no barbell back squat or conventional deadlift, no unsupported bent-over rowing, no cable kickbacks, no seated calf raise, no B-stance hip thrust, no second rear-delt or ab day, no behind-the-neck pressing or pulling, no static stretching before lifting, no foam rolling, no pre-workout mobility circuit, no post-activation potentiation protocol (Xu 2025, Sports Medicine, 62 studies: effect size 0.12, shrinking as training status rises, and not one measured outcome was hypertrophy), and no fourth ramping set on anything. Each of these was considered and rejected on the evidence, not on preference.
