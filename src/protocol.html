The Deficit Hypertrophy Protocol

Five lifting days and two cardio days, built to hold muscle and training performance through a calorie deficit without asking a forty-year-old lumbar spine to carry the load. Muscle retention is the floor; at the revised 250 kcal deficit, modest growth is a realistic aim rather than a bonus. Every exercise justified by unique stimulus, every dose traced to a citation, and a validation report that shows its working.

The one rule

The deficit is the number to keep small

Murphy & Koehler (2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports, meta-analysis and meta-regression of RCTs ≥3 weeks) found that training in an energy deficit impairs gains in lean mass but not strength, and that the regression crossed zero at roughly 500 kcal·day⁻¹ — beyond that deficit, lean mass gains stopped. That is the one finding here strong enough to govern a decision, and it governs the deficit, not the set count.

⚠️ Correction, 7 Sep 2026 — the second half of this rule has been withdrawn. This note previously claimed that Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer (2022, Eur J Appl Physiol, 15 studies) found holding at least 10 weekly sets per muscle was what tracked with little-to-no lean mass loss. The paper says close to the opposite. Its own words: volume could be calculated for only 4 of the 15 studies; the high-volume studies showing low-to-no lean mass loss were “mostly female”; and the stated conclusion is that “evidence is insufficient to conclude that a higher RT volume is better suited to spare lean mass during CR,” with the favourable signal confined to female athletes and women on average sparing more lean mass than men. The same group then tested it directly in trained males — Roth et al. (2023, Scand J Med Sci Sports, n=38, six weeks of restriction) — and found volume did not influence lean mass preservation (five versus three sets per exercise, no detected difference). Nait-Yahia et al. (2026, Eur J Clin Nutr, n=16) found the same for 12 versus 30 weekly sets, though strength favoured the higher dose. There is currently no evidence that a male lifter must hold 98–99 sets to retain muscle in a deficit.

One number governs this document: the deficit stays small. Revised 8 Sep 2026 to 250 kcal/day (range 200–300), down from a 500 kcal cap. That is the constraint with a meta-analysis directly behind it. The weekly set count is a default, not a second law — hold it when recovery allows, and cut it when recovery says so.

The instinct in a cut runs one way: training feels worse, sessions feel longer, and the response is to trim sets and add cardio. Half of that instinct is wrong and half is not. Adding cardio to rescue a stalling deficit is the inversion — the deficit is the variable to keep small, and cardio is not the lever that fixes it. But trimming sets is a legitimate response to genuine fatigue, and the evidence once cited here to forbid it does not support the ban. What the volume is actually defending is training stimulus and the logbook, and both can be defended at fewer sets if the alternative is degraded sessions.

Why hold 99 anyway, then? Because it is the workload already demonstrated as tolerable, and Pelland et al. (2026, 67 studies, 2,058 participants) puts the volume–hypertrophy slope in the positive direction with diminishing returns rather than a penalty. That is a reason to keep a productive workload, not a reason to defend it against your own recovery. The rule is now: change one thing at a time, and let symptoms and session quality — not the spreadsheet — decide which.

The second constraint shaping every page here is the lumbar spine. It is not treated as a mobility problem to be stretched away — it is treated as a loading budget. Axial load is spent deliberately on the two or three lifts that genuinely need it, and every other slot in the week is filled with a supported or machine-based alternative that buys the same hypertrophy at a lower spinal cost.

What changed from the previous revision, and why. The prior plan was audited against nine constraints and failed three of them outright. It had no periodisation at all — no accumulation, no deload. It had no stated progression rule — rep ranges and RIR but no trigger for adding load or sets. And seven muscles were trained once a week: chest, glutes, biceps, front delts, rear delts, abs, and each calf head individually. Weekly volume has barely moved. What moved is how it is distributed, and what tells you when to change it. The full audit is in the Validation tab.
01 — Warm-up

Six minutes upper, nine minutes lower, and nothing else

Why the warm-up is this short — the evidence

The evidence base for the conventional fifteen-minute ritual is thin to the point of embarrassment. Enes (2025, 29 trained lifters) compared two warm-up sets, one warm-up set, and none at all at 10-rep loads and found differences "negligible to small," recommending the specific warm-up be skipped for time efficiency. Ribeiro (2014) found the same at 80% 1RM — no difference in total reps or fatigue across four warm-up conditions. ⚠️ Enes was read as a preprint; the published version was not accessible. Treat as medium confidence.

Two things stop the answer being zero. Abad (2011) found adding a general warm-up raised leg-press 1RM 8.4% over a specific warm-up alone, and Barroso (2013) got +3% from a low-intensity general warm-up — and, importantly, found that long moderate-intensity warm-ups cost 4% of 1RM. Easy means easy. The second reason is spinal, and it is about risk rather than performance; it is why the lower-day warm-up is three minutes longer than the upper one. This matters here specifically because you train within about an hour of waking — the whole morning-spine section below is load-bearing rather than precautionary, and it is also why the RDL sits fourth on Day 2 rather than first.

⚠️ The one injury-specific rule, and the only reason any mobility work survives in this plan. No held lumbar-flexion stretches in your first waking hour: toe touches, standing forward folds, seated hamstring stretches, child's pose, or any sustained rounded-back foam-rolling position. Adams & Dolan (1996) found five minutes of sustained flexion cut the spine's bending resistance by 42% across 45 cadaveric segments, and Snook's sham-controlled RCT (1998) found restricting early-morning lumbar flexion produced a 33% reduction in pain intensity (95% CI 11–55%). The mechanism is duration-dependent creep, not flexion itself — a controlled RDL is a different exposure from a 60-second hold, and rapid flexion actually increased bending resistance 10–15%. It is the held position to eliminate, not hip hinging. Scope, added 8 Sep 2026: Snook studied 85 people with persistent or recurrent low-back pain, so this is a reasonable precaution given your symptom history — not a universal law of mornings, and not a clock that clears you to hinge once it runs out. A separate systematic review (Saraceni 2020) found low-quality evidence and no established link between lifting flexion and back pain at all. Start gently when stiff, and modify what repeatedly provokes symptoms.
Higher-yield than any stretch in this section: if you sit hunched in a car on the way to the gym, you arrive with degraded protective reflexes. Sánchez-Zuriaga (2010) found an hour of flexed sitting raised back-muscle reflex latency from 60 ms to 96 ms, and muscle fatigue alone did not do that. Stand upright for two or three minutes before you start.
02 — The program

Five lifting days

How every session is ordered, and the rest periods

Order within a session is by priority: whatever you most want to progress goes first, while fresh. Not because it grows more — Nunes (2021, Eur J Sport Sci, 11-study meta-analysis) found no effect of order on hypertrophy — but because it accumulates more load over months.

How every session is ordered — the rule, and what it rests on

Sequencing in earlier drafts was inherited rather than reasoned, and it showed: calves sat in the middle of Day 2, and Day 4 ran a leg extension before a lunge. Both days have been rebuilt against an explicit rule, applied in this order:

  1. The heavy tier goes first. Nunes (2021, Eur J Sport Sci, 11 studies) found the exercise placed first accumulates the most strength — hypertrophy is unaffected by order, so first position is spent on whatever you most want to get stronger at.
  2. The day’s priority lift goes first even when it is single-joint. Same meta-analysis: single-joint exercises gain nearly twice as much from first position as multi-joint ones. This is why Day 2 opens with a leg curl.
  3. Multi-joint before single-joint — but only within a shared muscle. Pre-exhaustion costs 10–30% load on the following compound with no hypertrophy return (Fisher 2014; Nunes 2021). That penalty only exists if the two movements share tissue, so a leg extension can sit ahead of an RDL and cost nothing.
  4. Calves and trunk go last, always. They interfere with nothing and nothing interferes with them, so they are the cheapest thing to displace when the clock runs out.
  5. Within the isolation block, the arm exercise goes last — not first. This looks like it violates rule 2, and it is the one place where a competing principle wins. On the two upper days the elbow flexors or extensors are already pre-fatigued by seven compound sets before any isolation starts, so moving the curl or the extension earlier does not find them fresh. What it would do is remove the ten minutes of unrelated delt work that currently sits between the compounds and the arm work — and that gap functions as an extended rest interval, which is at worst neutral — though note (8 Sep 2026) that Singer could not sub-analyse trained lifters, so "favourable for trained lifters" is not a claim either source supports. So the arm work goes last to buy recovery, not because it matters least.
  6. One documented exception: the RDL is placed fourth on Day 2 despite being the day’s heaviest hinge. Amended 8 Sep 2026: this used to be justified by morning lumbar-creep evidence plus deliberate load reduction from pre-fatigued hamstrings. Neither claim survives scrutiny as a safety argument — see the Day 2 table. The placement is retained on tolerance and habit, and it is a preference rather than a rule. Moving the RDL earlier, when technique is freshest, is an equally defensible choice.

On the two lower days mixing anterior and posterior work — this is deliberate. Day 2 is posterior-led and Day 4 is quad-led: the naming describes which muscle owns the heavy tier and the first three slots, not an exclusive territory. Each day then carries two or three cheap machine sets for the other half, placed last. The reason is arithmetic, not preference — with only two lower sessions, a strict anterior/posterior split forces every lower-body muscle to one exposure a week, which is exactly the failure this revision exists to fix. Quads get 2 sets on Day 2 and hamstrings get 3 on Day 4 for that reason alone, and both are leg-machine isolation with no bearing on the session's main work. (Earlier drafts named these days "Posterior + Core" and "Anterior," which implied an exclusivity the plan never had. The names are now accurate; the structure has not changed.)

Rest periods: compounds 150 s, isolation 90 s — two options only, simplified 6 Sep 2026. ⚠️ Justification re-based 8 Sep 2026 — the prescription is unchanged, the reason for it was wrong. This previously said Grgic (2017) and Singer (2024) favour longer rest for trained lifters. Singer et al. (2024, Front Sports Act Living) states verbatim that it "did not detect appreciable differences in hypertrophy when resting >90 s between sets," and of its 9 studies only 3 used trained participants — the authors say outright that insufficient data existed to sub-analyse them. Grgic's stated conclusion is equivocal. There is no hypertrophy case for 150 s.

The rest stays anyway, on a defensible basis. Longo et al. Rest is not the variable — completed reps are. 150 s on the heavy tier protects the reps and the load progression this document is anchored to; 90 s on isolation sits at the boundary Singer identifies as the last defensible one. Shorten either only as far as the logbook shows reps holding.

The counting rule, stated once and applied everywhere. Rewritten 8 Sep 2026. Every set-count in this document now follows exactly this, with no exceptions and no per-muscle conventions:

1. A set counts 1.0 for every muscle the exercise trains as a primary mover. Sets are never subdivided between muscles. A Smith lunge set is one quad set and one glute set. An RDL set is one hamstring set and one glute set.
2. A set counts 0.5 for every muscle that works hard as a synergist — presses feed triceps and front delts, vertical and horizontal pulls feed biceps, horizontal pulls feed rear delts, the shoulder press feeds side delts, the hack squat and lunge feed the glutes.
3. Sets where a muscle only stabilises earn nothing — which is why abs and calves carry no fractional credit.
4. The muscle rows therefore sum to more than 99, and that is correct. A set can train two muscles. The previous total of exactly 98 was produced by splitting the lunge 1 quad : 3 glute and by treating the RDL as a direct hamstring but only a fractional glute exercise — two ad-hoc conventions that existed to force the arithmetic to balance, and which between them understated quads by 3 sets and glutes by 4.

Why two calf exercises on one day was the wrong design, and why one seated raise is still in the week. The plan ran seated and standing raises on both lower days, on the standard reasoning that the soleus only loads with the knee bent and therefore needs its own exercise. That reasoning is not mechanically true — corrected 8 Sep 2026. The soleus is a plantarflexor at any knee angle (it does not cross the knee); Kinoshita measured no detectable difference in soleus growth between positions.

Kinoshita et al. (2023, Frontiers in Physiology) trained 14 adults for 12 weeks in a within-subject unilateral model, one leg standing and one leg seated, 5 × 10 at 70% 1RM twice weekly. Standing grew the lateral gastrocnemius +12.4% vs +1.7% and the medial +9.2% vs +0.6% (both p ≤ 0.002, large effects). Soleus grew 2.1% standing vs 2.9% seated — trivial and non-significant (d = 0.2). The authors concluded standing was "by far more effective," and attributed it to training the gastrocnemius at long muscle length. ⚠️ 14 untrained participants, no control group. Within-subject design makes the comparison strong; the untrained sample makes the transfer to you weaker. Medium confidence.

So running both raises on both lower days was not "too much" — it was buying one muscle that the standing raise already grows just as well, at the price of an extra exercise on both lower days. That version was cut, taking calves from 8 weekly sets across two exercises to 6 across one. The seated raise was then reinstated on Day 2 for a different job (see its row), with the standing raise on Day 4 — still 6 weekly sets, now across two exercises, one per lower day. Why glute volume was raised above the first draft’s 7 sets — a correction, and the reasoning that forced it. The first version of this document capped glutes below band and justified it as "the lumbar constraint being paid for." That conflated two different things: glute training and axially expensive glute training. The constraint was never a reason to hold glute volume down — only a reason to spend axial load deliberately: on supported or machine movements wherever they buy the same stimulus, and knowingly on the fifth hip-thrust set and the Smith lunge (below).

And the direction of the evidence runs the other way. Gluteus medius weakness and hip extensor weakness are consistently more prevalent in people with chronic low back pain (Cooper et al. 2016, Eur Spine J, cross-sectional; Sadler et al. 2019, systematic review), and the gluteus maximus is a primary synergist for lumbar extension — so a weak one shifts that work onto the erectors. The intervention evidence is weaker but points the same way: a meta-analysis of 8 RCTs, n=508 found hip strengthening reduced pain (MD −0.66, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.48) and disability (SMD −0.81, 95% CI −1.53 to −0.10) in the short term. ⚠️ Graded "very low certainty" by its own authors — high risk of bias, no medium- or long-term effect, and at least one RCT found no benefit from adding hip strengthening to standard care. Causation is also unresolved: pain may inhibit the glutes rather than weak glutes causing pain.

Lower-body priority: plantarflexors first, quads second, hip extensors third — and one stated reason that the evidence refutes. Upper-body priority is straightforward: back, chest and shoulders, because that is the physique goal. Lower body was set against three stated aims — running, long-term health, and "supporting upper-body gains." The evidence separates those cleanly.

Plantarflexors, soleus especially. The soleus is the largest single contributor to vertical support and forward propulsion in running (Hamner & Delp 2013), triceps surae is roughly 25% of running's metabolic cost, and (the “loads only with the knee bent” claim was withdrawn 8 Sep 2026 — see Day 2) 14 weeks of plantarflexor training raised Achilles tendon-aponeurosis stiffness ~16% and cut metabolic cost ~4% (Albracht & Arampatzis 2013, n=13–14) — corrected from 31% on 8 Sep 2026. Medium confidence. The seated raise came back for a different reason: it is a second calf exposure at a different position, with no spinal load — the running case above is a secondary benefit of heavy calf work, not what puts it in the week. The soleus does not cross the knee, so it does not need the bent knee to be trained (Kinoshita 2023). “First” ranks the evidence behind the three aims, not weekly sets. Quadriceps. The strongest longevity case of any lower-body measure: in the Health ABC cohort (Newman et al. 2006, n≈2,292) quadriceps strength predicted mortality while leg muscle mass did not, and the association survived adjustment for muscle size. NHANES analyses put each 15 N of knee-extensor strength at 7% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.93, CI 0.91–0.95). Quadriceps is also the fastest-atrophying lower-limb group with age, driven by type II fibre loss (Nilwik 2013; Mitchell 2012). ⚠️ All observational, mostly in adults 65+. A prognostic marker, not proven causation, and you are 40. The corollary matters: train legs for strength expression, not just size. The mass-focused lens that suits your upper body is the wrong one here.

Hip extensors, third and hedged. Honestly the thinnest of the three — see the injury note below.
⚠️ Three things the evidence contradicts, stated plainly because they were part of the brief.

1. "A strong lower body supports upper-body muscle gains." This is refuted, not merely unproven. The mechanism behind it is the acute-hormone hypothesis, and it has been directly tested. West et al. (2010, J Appl Physiol) ran 15 weeks of unilateral elbow-flexor training with and without a prior high-volume leg bout and found no difference in hypertrophy or strength. West et al. (2009, J Physiol), Mitchell et al. (2013, PLoS ONE) and Morton et al. (2016) agree; Phillips (2012) titled his commentary "chasing a hormonal ghost." The single supporting study (Rønnestad 2011) had n=9, showed the effect at one of several measurement sites, was publicly questioned, and has not been replicated. Since recovery capacity is finite and in a deficit it is reduced, excess leg volume plausibly costs you upper-body progress. Train legs on their own merits — running, healthspan, function — not as an upper-body accessory.

2. Lifting will not raise your VO₂max. The running-economy meta-analysis (Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024, Sports Medicine, 31 trials, 652 runners) found high-load strength training improved economy (ES −0.266) and time-trial performance (ES −0.469) while VO₂max, vVO₂max and lactate threshold were all unchanged (p > 0.23). The lifting works on the economy and force-production axis; the running does the aerobic work. Both are worth doing, for different reasons.

3. Strength training is not reliable injury-proofing for running. Peterson et al. (2022, Sports Med Open), 30 prospective studies, 3,404 non-elite runners, tested over 100 candidate risk factors. The only significant strength finding was knee-extension strength at SMD −0.19 — which the authors themselves call trivial and possibly not clinically important. Plantarflexor, hamstring and hip-abductor strength: all null. Hip strength and patellofemoral pain is null prospectively even though it looks strong cross-sectionally (Rathleff 2014) — the weakness follows the pain rather than causing it. The best-supported modifiable risk factor in running injury is training-load management and prior injury history, not muscle strength. Lift for economy and healthspan; do not sell it to yourself as injury insurance.

V — Four-day variant

Nearly the same work, in four sessions

What this variant is, how the week is scheduled, and the evidence it rests on
The verdict first: yes, and it costs you nothing measurable in hypertrophy. This variant is not a reduced plan. It carries 98 weekly hard sets against the five-day's 99, redistributed across four sessions instead of five. Nothing was cut. Since 17 Sep 2026 the two plans differ by one set in two places, both at the trainee's request: side delts 8 here against 9 in the five-day, biceps 8 against 7, and — since 18 Sep 2026 — abs 5 against 6. Every other muscle is at the same direct-set count. Every priority muscle keeps two exposures, and no muscle in any session exceeds 11 fractional sets (quads on Friday: hack squat 4 + lunge 4 + leg extension 3), which is the same as the five-day plan's own peak (quads on Day 4). Every other muscle is at 8.5 or below, against the five-day's 9 (chest, Day 3).

What it costs is session length, and that number is not small. Weekly lifting time is essentially unchanged — the work is identical, so redistributing it across fewer days does not shrink it. What disappears is one gym trip and one warm-up. The cost is session length: the two upper days run ~92 and ~91 minutes against the five-day’s 58–66. Everything is sequential straight sets with full prescribed rest; nothing is paired, nothing is compressed. The saving comes from losing a session's warm-up, travel and setup overhead, not from working faster.
The drop markers, and how to use them mid-session. Three exercises in each session carry a drop 1st / 2nd / 3rd tag in their sub-label, and the same tag is prepended to the exercise note in Hevy so it is on screen while you train. Both plans carry them, on the same criteria — the five-day’s were extended from three tagged exercises to a full per-session order on 6 Sep 2026 so the two are read the same way. Note that §06’s weekly landmine is a different order on purpose: these markers answer “short on time”, the landmine answers “under-recovered”. It means shave one set, in that order — not skip the exercise. Drop 1st only if the session is genuinely running long; work down the order and stop as soon as you are back on time.

Cut sets, never intensity — and that half is evidence-backed. Bickel et al. (2011, MSSE, 70 adults, 32 weeks) cut trained subjects to a third and even a ninth of their previous dose while holding load constant at 8–12RM: in the 20–35 group both doses preserved the hypertrophy already built, and the one-third dose added more. Spiering et al. (2021) reach the same place from the review literature — roughly two sessions a week at 2–3 sets per exercise maintains, provided intensity holds. So a dropped set is cheap and a dropped load is not. ⚠️ One caution that lands on you specifically: Bickel’s 60–75 group did not preserve hypertrophy on the reduced dose, only strength. At 40 you sit between the two cohorts, so treat the drop markers as an occasional release valve rather than a routine setting.

What the literature does not tell you is which exercise to cut — no study has ever compared it. The order below is therefore built from two criteria this document can defend. First, cushioning: under Pelland’s fractional-set model a muscle with heavy indirect coverage loses least from a dropped direct set, which is why the leg extension goes before the knee raise — quads collect fractional volume from the hack squat and the lunge, while abs and calves collect essentially none, so their sets are a pure loss. Second, expected value: where a lift’s own justification is weak it goes first regardless of cushioning, which is the hip abduction’s case below.

Two hard rules, unchanged from §06: never touch the heavy tier, and never take a muscle below two weekly exposures. The order was built against them — dropping all three in any session costs three sets and leaves every muscle at 2× and inside its band. Everything untagged is not negotiable for time: the heavy lifts, the RDL, the seated leg curl, both chest presses, the pec deck, the shoulder press, both calf raises and the overhead extension are each either the priority lift of their day or the only exposure of a stimulus nothing else in the week provides.

Why the first cut on Friday is the hip abduction. It is the plan’s weakest-evidenced inclusion and this document should say so plainly. The rationale is lumbar insurance, not size — gluteus medius weakness is more prevalent in chronic low back pain (Cooper 2016; Sadler 2019), and hip strengthening reduced pain (MD −0.66) and disability (SMD −0.81) across 8 RCTs, n=508. But that meta-analysis is graded “very low certainty” by its own authors, shows no medium- or long-term effect, and cannot resolve whether weak glutes cause pain or pain inhibits glutes. Meanwhile Peterson et al. (2022, 30 prospective studies, 3,404 runners) found hip-abductor strength flatly null for injury risk. Three sets is a cheap hedge on thin evidence — worth keeping, and the right thing to lose first.

Why the frequency drop is free — the four findings this rests on

1. Frequency has no detectable independent effect on hypertrophy once weekly volume is held constant. Pelland et al. (2026, Sports Medicine 56(2):481–505; 67 studies, 2,058 participants) ran Bayesian meta-regressions on volume and frequency separately, adjusting each for the other. For volume and hypertrophy the posterior probability that the slope exceeds zero was 100%. For frequency and hypertrophy it was 91.3%, with a credible interval that contains the null (β = 0.32% muscle size per session, 95% CrI −0.14 to 0.82). Their own summary is that this is "compatible with negligible effects." Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger (2019, J Sports Sci, 25 studies, n=836) reached the same conclusion by a different route. High confidence. This is the single most replicated finding in the frequency literature, and it is the load-bearing claim for this entire variant.

2. Frequency does matter for strength — which is why nothing was moved to 1×. The same Pelland regression found a 100% posterior probability for frequency's effect on strength, rising from a fractional frequency of 1 (ES = 12.72% [95% CrI 10.57, 15.05]) to 2 (ES = 17.32% [14.34, 20.56]), with accelerating diminishing returns beyond that. The practical reading is blunt: the second exposure is worth having and the third is not. Every muscle that had two exposures in the five-day plan still has two here. The three that had one — rear delts, abs, gluteus medius — still have one, for the reasons already given in Weekly totals. The only muscle demoted is side delts, 3× → 2×, and the five-day document already states that its third exposure existed for session balance rather than growth. High confidence.

3. Per-session concentration per muscle is the more useful thing to look at than session totals — and this variant is no worse on it. Rewritten 8 Sep 2026. This argument used to be run as a threshold test: Remmert et al. (2025) put the point of undetectable outcome superiority at ~11 fractional sets per muscle per session, and the four-day was defended as clearing it. That framing is withdrawn — the figure is a limit of statistical resolution in an unreviewed preprint, not a ceiling a plan must clear, and it has been demoted to a heuristic throughout this document.

What survives is the comparison itself, which does not need the threshold. The five-day runs chest 9 / 3 across its two exposures; the four-day runs 5 / 7, which is a more even distribution of the same work. Both plans share the same most-concentrated slot after the 8 Sep recount: quads at 11 sets on the second lower day. Rear delts sit at 6.5 in their single session (5 direct + 0.5 × the one horizontal row), corrected here from a previously stated 8.5 that wrongly credited the pulldown sets. So condensing five days into four did not push local concentration up in this case. That is a real and checkable point in the variant's favour; it is not evidence that either arrangement grows more muscle. Medium confidence in the distribution comparison, low in any threshold claim.

4. One heavy lift per session, which is the five-day's own rule and the earlier draft broke it. The Loading section specifies "exactly one exercise per lifting day, always first, always supported." The paired version of this variant put the lat pulldown and the machine chest press on the same day, both on the heavy tier — a straightforward violation that pairing disguised. Straight sets made it obvious: two heavy four-set lifts with full rests and two ramps each cost roughly 32 minutes before anything else happens. The heavy tier is now split one per upper day, pulldown on Upper A and chest press on Upper B, exactly as the five-day distributes it.

The strongest argument against this variant, stated properly

Chris Beardsley's frequency–volume critique says the whole literature above is biased toward finding null. His argument: most frequency studies gave their higher-frequency arms inadequate recovery between sessions, because full recovery from 6–8 sets to failure plausibly takes more than four days. If the high-frequency groups were training partially recovered — with reduced motor unit recruitment and lower mechanical tension per set — then their results are handicapped, and the observed null is an artefact rather than a finding. He argues further that the first set of a workout is worth more than subsequent sets, which if true means more sessions per week is inherently better and the four-day plan is giving something up.

Taken seriously, this points away from the variant, not toward it. It deserves to be here for exactly that reason. Three things temper it: (1) it is a paywalled newsletter analysis, not peer-reviewed and not a published meta-analysis — it does not carry the evidentiary weight of Pelland or Ramos-Campo, and grading it as though it does would be dishonest in the other direction; (2) its own logic cuts against extreme frequencies most, and this variant sits at 2× per muscle — the frequency Beardsley's framework and the meta-regressions both endorse; (3) the "first set is worth more" premise, if true, argues against the five-day plan too, since that plan also runs each muscle 2×, just on a different calendar. The only place it bites specifically is side delts, 3× → 2×. That is one muscle, ten sets, and it is the single thing this variant genuinely wagers. ⚠️ Non-peer-reviewed dissent from a credible analyst. Low-medium confidence, high relevance.

The mechanism runs slightly against the outcome data — say so

MacDougall et al. (1995, Can J Appl Physiol) found muscle protein synthesis elevated 50% at 4 h and 109% at 24 h post-exercise, declining rapidly thereafter and almost back to baseline by 36 h. Damas et al. (2016, J Physiol) qualified this for trained lifters: the early MPS response is dominated by damage repair, and once damage is attenuated by ~10 weeks of training, MPS is still elevated at 48 h and that elevation is the one that correlates with hypertrophy.

Read literally, that mechanism favours the five-day plan. Two exposures 72 h apart leave more of the week at baseline than exposures 48–96 h apart do. It is cited here because it is the honest counterweight, and then set aside for a specific reason: MPS time-course is a proxy, and acute proxies have a poor record of predicting chronic hypertrophy — the acute-hormone hypothesis is the cautionary example this document already covers in Weekly totals. Where a proxy and an outcome-level meta-regression of 67 studies disagree, the outcome wins. High confidence in the MPS measurements themselves; low confidence that they predict the 12-week result.

Two things that decided the design rather than the verdict

The four-day is volume-matched rather than volume-reduced — but the reason is weaker than this section used to claim. Revised 7 Sep 2026. Roth et al. (2022, Eur J Appl Physiol) does observe that studies reducing training volume as a diet progressed appeared to fare worse than those maintaining or increasing it, and that ≥10-set programmes showed low-to-no lean mass loss. Both observations are real and both are reported inside a review whose stated conclusion is that “evidence is insufficient to conclude that a higher RT volume is better suited to spare lean mass during CR” — with the favourable signal concentrated in female athletes. The same group's subsequent RCT in trained males found no volume effect at all. So the honest framing: matching volume across the two splits keeps the comparison clean and changes only one variable, which is a good experimental reason. It is not evidence that a shorter four-day plan would have cost muscle. That option is on the table if the schedule demands it. ⚠️ Low-to-medium confidence. The earlier “medium, and the direction is consistent across every source I found” overstated a literature that includes two direct nulls in males.

What the evidence does not say, stated before the tables rather than after them. No study has compared a four-day and a five-day split at 98–99 weekly sets, in a modest deficit, in a 40-year-old. Per muscle it is fine, and per muscle is what Remmert measured; the worst slot here is 11 fractional sets (quads on Friday, identical to the five-day) against a ~11 ceiling, and every other muscle is at 8.5 or below. But nobody has tested whether total session volume across all muscles has its own independent cost once you pass some threshold. I went looking specifically for a session-duration ceiling and could not find one: no meta-analysis or trial in the hypertrophy literature identifies a length at which sessions stop working, and the umbrella review of hypertrophy variables (Bernárdez-Vázquez et al. 2022, Front Sports Act Living) does not list session duration as a programming variable at all. That is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence, and it remains the single largest unknown in the variant.

3. You lose the five-day's error tolerance. At five sessions, missing one costs 20% of the week. At four it costs 25%, and because each muscle now has exactly two exposures, a missed session takes a muscle to one — the frequency step Pelland found actually does matter. The rule: a missed session gets made up on Wednesday or the weekend, not skipped. A four-day plan that becomes a three-day plan twice a month is worse than the five-day, and that is the realistic failure mode, not overtraining.

How to trial it, and what would send you back

Run one full four-week block, not two weeks. The first week will be slower purely from unfamiliarity with a reordered session, and judging on it would be judging the logistics rather than the plan.

The comparison is load × reps on six anchor lifts, measured against the same week of the previous five-day block: lat pulldown, machine chest press, seated leg curl, hack squat, machine hip thrust, machine lateral raise. Weigh in on the same schedule and hold the deficit constant — changing the training split and the calories in the same block would make the result uninterpretable, which is the most common way trials like this waste a month.

The abort condition, stated concretely so it is not a judgement call in the moment. Go back to the five-day if either of these happens:

(a) Load or reps on Thursday's cable pushdown or hammer curl — the last two exercises of the 81-minute session — decline at matched load for two consecutive weeks while Monday's curl and overhead extension are holding. That is the signature of a session that is too long, and it is distinguishable from general deficit fatigue precisely because Monday would be unaffected.

(b) Any two of the six anchor lifts decline at matched load for two consecutive weeks. That is systemic rather than positional — in which case the first thing to try is not reverting but moving the Iso-Lateral Low Row to Friday, which takes about nine minutes off Thursday.

Neither soreness nor perceived difficulty is on this list. Soreness is not a readiness signal anywhere else in this document and it is not one here. The warning sign is performance at matched load.
What switching back costs: nothing. Both plans use the same exercises, the same loading tiers, the same four-week block structure, the same progression rules and the same nutrition. The variant is a re-sequencing, not a second program — so a failed trial costs you one block of slightly different scheduling and no lost training. That asymmetry is the actual argument for trying it: the downside is a month of equivalent training, and the upside is one gym trip back every week for the rest of the diet, and a third rest day.
04 — Loading

Three tiers, and the reason for each is fatigue, not growth

Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger (2017, JSCR, 21 studies) compared ≤60% 1RM against >60% 1RM with all sets taken to momentary failure and found hypertrophy was equivalent across the range, while 1RM strength gains significantly favoured heavy loads. Lasevicius (2018, Eur J Sport Sci, within-subject, volume-equated) found 40%, 60% and 80% 1RM produced similar hypertrophy while 20% did not — placing the lower boundary at roughly 30–40%, not lower.

If load does not determine hypertrophy, then load should be chosen for what it does determine: joint stress, spinal exposure, and how much of the session's fatigue budget the set spends.

That is the entire logic of the three tiers below. Heavy work is confined to the one supported machine at the front of each session, where the load buys strength and tendon adaptation and the support removes the axial penalty that normally makes heavy work expensive for a forty-year-old lumbar spine. Light work carries the lengthened-position stimulus and the metabolite-heavy finishing sets, where the load is trivially low and the set can be taken to genuine failure at almost no systemic cost.

Heavy ~75–85% 1RM · 6–8 reps · 2 RIR · rest 150 s

Exactly one exercise per lifting day, always supported: the pulldown (D1), the machine chest press (D3), the hack squat (D4). First in the session too, with one standing exception — corrected 6 Sep 2026, because this line read “always first” and Day 3 has not worked that way for some time. Day 3 opens with the incline DB press by the trainee’s preference, and the heavy chest press runs second. That costs little: order has no effect on hypertrophy (Nunes 2021, ES 0.03, p = 0.862), the chest is warm by the time the heavy set arrives — which is why its ramp is 1 set rather than 2 — and what first position buys is strength accrual on whichever lift holds it. The preference is the reason; it does not need an evidential one. The four-day’s Thursday now does the same — the flat dumbbell press leads and the heavy chest press runs second (17 Sep 2026). Day 2's leg curl and Day 5 carry no heavy tier at all. This is where strength gain lives — Schoenfeld 2017 found heavy load matters for 1RM even where it does not for size.

Moderate ~65–75% 1RM · 8–12 reps · 1–2 RIR · rest 150 s

The second and third compounds: rows, incline press, hip thrust, shoulder press, Smith lunge, RDL. The workhorse tier — enough load to progress on, light enough that three or four sets do not blow the session's recovery budget.

Light ~40–60% 1RM · 12–20 reps · 0–1 RIR · rest 90 s

All isolation, all lengthened-position work, all cable and machine single-joint movements. Every set taken to failure in the program lives in this tier. It is where volume is cheap — and in a deficit, cheap volume is the only kind worth adding.

Two exceptions, both deliberate. The calf raises run at 6–8 reps with a three-second eccentric — heavier and slower than their isolation status would suggest, because tendon adaptation is driven by loading magnitude rather than rep count (Bohm). The Romanian deadlift runs at 6–8 reps but at 2–3 RIR rather than 2 — it is the only barbell lift in the program and the only one where a missed rep is a spinal event rather than a training one.
05 — The block

Three weeks flat, one week down

Volume is constant for three weeks, then halved for one. Nothing is added mid-block. The only thing that changes week to week is load — which is what makes a stalled lift mean something.

On "priority muscles," stated honestly. Allocating more sets to some muscles than others is a goal-driven choice, not an evidence-based one. No study has compared a volume-prioritised programme against an evenly-distributed one. No meta-analysis has established muscle-specific volume landmarks — Pelland et al. (2026, 67 studies, n=2,058) classifies sets as direct or indirect relative to the measured muscle and does not stratify by muscle group at all, and the ACSM 2026 position stand says only that hypertrophy was enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk) — the higher-volume comparison group that did better, not a per-muscle minimum or target. Published MEV/MAV/MRV-per-muscle tables are practitioner heuristics with no peer-reviewed validation.

What is evidence-based is the mechanism the choice runs on: a volume dose–response with diminishing returns that appears to operate similarly across muscles. Directing more sets at a muscle is therefore a reasonable way to bias growth toward it — but which muscles is a preference, and it should be the trainee’s preference rather than the author’s. Here it is: back, chest and shoulders, stated by the trainee as the physique goal. Quads are the one addition made on evidence rather than preference — the knee-extensor strength/mortality association and the age-related type II atrophy data, both set out in Weekly totals. Calves are prioritised too, but through load rather than sets, because the running-economy case is about tendon stiffness and strength, not size. And note what "priority" now means in practice, since volume is flat: no muscle gets extra sets at any point in the block. Priority shows up as first position in the session, ownership of the heavy tier, and being the last thing cut when the clock runs out — all of which are free, unlike sets. One caution worth recording: the only muscle-specific volume signal in the literature, a subgroup finding in Baz-Valle (2022), favoured triceps at 20+ sets while quadriceps showed no such benefit — which is close to the opposite of the usual priority list. It is underpowered and is a difference in significance rather than a tested interaction, so it settles nothing. It is here because it is the only muscle-specific evidence that exists.

Week 1 Calibrate — calibration calories

99 sets. Calibration calories (set in your nutrition plan), not deficit. Establish honest loads across every exercise. If in doubt, go lighter — you are calibrating, not testing.

Week 2 Same sets, deficit starts

99 sets, unchanged. The deficit starts this week; calories come from your nutrition plan. Progress load only — double progression on every lift that earned it.

Week 3 Same sets again

99 sets, unchanged. Load progression continues. This is the hardest week of the block; expect the logbook to move slower than week 2.

Week 4 Deload

Sets halved (~49). Load unchanged. Every set at 4–5 RIR. Nothing to failure. If you run your own cardio, skip any interval work that week; run easy or bike.

Confidence: low-to-medium, and here is the conflict. Deloading is near-universal in practice and thinly evidenced in the literature. Coleman et al. (2024, PeerJ 12:e16777, n=39, 9-week supervised RT) inserted a one-week cessation at the midpoint and found no difference in hypertrophy but worse lower-body strength in the deload group, plus slightly better readiness-to-train scores in the continuous group. Taken at face value, that argues against deloading. Three things stop it settling the question: it tested complete cessation rather than a reduced-volume week; the subjects were not in an energy deficit; and no trial has tested deloading in the population this document is written for. Per the conservative-option rule, the plan keeps a reduced-volume deload — which is not what Coleman tested — and keeps load unchanged, which is the specific variable Coleman found cessation cost. Added 31 Aug 2026: the reduced-volume distinction this plan leans on has now been tested directly, if not in the right population. Pancar et al. (2026, Scientific Reports) randomised limbs within-subject in 19 untrained men over 8 weeks, comparing a genuinely aggressive reduced-volume deload — one session of two sets, against two sessions of six to eight — and found no time × condition interaction (p = 0.239–0.955, ηp² 0.001–0.076). That is a harder cut than this plan’s halving, and it cost nothing. It does not validate the choice in trained lifters, but it does remove the specific worry Coleman raised, because Coleman tested cessation and Pancar tested reduction. Confidence accordingly moves from low to low-to-medium. One thing that remains pure convention and should be named as such: the four-week cadence has no evidential support whatsoever — no trial has ever compared one deload frequency against another. 06 — Progression

Double progression, and three rules for what to change

Progressive overload now has a direct trial — added 8 Sep 2026. This section previously cited Pelland for volume and Barsuhn/Moreno for why not to add sets, but carried no citation for load progression itself — the thing the entire rule rests on. Kassiano et al. (2026, MSSE 58:1556–1565) randomised each arm of 55 participants to progressive overload, non-progressive overload, or non-exercise control across 8 weeks of elbow extension: triceps growth was +22.9% with progression versus +11.6% without (control +1.8%, all p < 0.001) — roughly double, in a within-participant design that controls for genetics. ⚠️ Untrained young women, 8 weeks. The non-progressive group still grew substantially, and the authors note that whether non-progressive training keeps working over longer periods is unknown.

The rule, in one line: when you hit the top of the prescribed rep range on every working set at the target RIR, add the smallest available load increment next session and rebuild through the range from the bottom.

Worked example. Day 3 machine chest press is 4 × 6–8 at 2 RIR. You log 8, 8, 8, 8 at 2 RIR. Next session: add one pin or the smallest plate, expect 6, 6, 6, 6, and work back up over the following two or three sessions. You log 8, 8, 7, 6 instead? Stay at that load. The set count and the RIR never move to make the load move — that is what separates this from just grinding.

The three placement rules, and what backs each

1 — Cardio never precedes lifting. Not on any day, not in any version of the week.

The evidence behind this

Schumann (2022, Sports Medicine, 43 studies) found that sequence did not meaningfully affect hypertrophy or maximal strength — regardless of modality, frequency, training status, age above or below 40, or whether the sessions were same-session, same-day or separate days. But the same analysis found explosive strength attenuated at SMD −0.28 (95% CI −0.48 to −0.08) when the two were combined — an effect size, not a 28% reduction; unit error corrected 8 Sep 2026, most strongly within a single session. Since the cost of putting cardio second is zero and the cost of putting it first is a possible decrement in the session that is doing the actual muscle-building, the rule is free. Confidence: high for "it does not matter much," high for "second is never worse."

2 — Cycling and incline walking are the default; running is confined to one session and can be dropped entirely. The prescription is unchanged; the reason for it changed on 31 Aug 2026.

⚠️ This rule used to be justified as the conservative bet against interference. That justification no longer holds — the modality evidence has flipped direction. Wilson (2012) found interference from running but not cycling, and this document locked cycling on that basis. Two later analyses point the other way. Chen et al. (2024, J Exerc Sci Fit, systematic review + network meta-analysis, 40 studies, 841 participants) ranked HIIT running as the least-interfering modality (lower-body strength SMD −0.08, the smallest deficit of any combination), with muscle CSA unharmed by either modality (HIIT-running SMD 0.15, MICT-cycling 0.07). Sabag et al. (2018, J Sports Sci) found the same direction in sub-analysis: cycling-HIIT ES −0.377 versus running ES −0.176. Schumann (2022) still finds no modality effect at all. Three positions, no resolution — and every magnitude is trivial, which is the genuinely useful finding. So the bike stays, but on honest grounds: it is a lumbar-sparing and joint-impact decision, not an interference one. Cycling has near-zero eccentric load and no impact, which matters at forty and matters more in a deficit where connective-tissue recovery is reduced. The one weekly running slot survives for what cycling cannot give — weight-bearing bone and tendon loading. That reasoning was always the stronger half of the argument; it is now the whole of it.
The evidence behind this — and a genuine conflict

This is the one place in the document where two good sources disagree. Wilson (2012, JSCR, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes) found that resistance training performed concurrently with running, but not cycling, produced significant decrements in both hypertrophy and strength — attributed to running's greater eccentric load and muscle damage. Schumann (2022), a decade later and with more studies, found no modality effect at all.

Neither is dismissible. Wilson is older and its running studies skew toward higher volumes than prescribed here; Schumann is larger and more recent but pools across a wider range of designs. The choice locked here: default to cycling and incline walking. If Schumann is right, that choice costs nothing — the modality is irrelevant and you have lost only running specificity. If Wilson is right, it protects the primary outcome. There is no scenario where preferring the bike is the wrong bet, which is what makes it the conservative option. Running survives in exactly one weekly slot, and only because of what it offers that cycling cannot: weight-bearing bone and tendon loading, which matters more at 40 than at 25. Confidence: medium — the modality question is genuinely unsettled.

3 — Zone 2, not intervals, for almost all of it. One optional interval session a week, maximum, and none in the deload week.

The evidence behind this

Two independent reasons, neither of which is "Zone 2 burns more fat" — it does not, and the fat-loss work is done by the deficit. First, interference in the Wilson analysis correlated with the frequency and duration of endurance work, and high-intensity work costs disproportionately more recovery per minute. Second, and more practically: cardiorespiratory fitness predicts mortality with no observed upper limit (Mandsager, 122,007 patients) — so there is a real reason to keep some high-intensity work in the week, but no reason to buy it at the price of the lifting. One session is the compromise. Confidence: medium-high.

Why the total is capped where it is. About 28% of exercise-induced energy expenditure disappears to compensation (Careau) — mostly through NEAT falling. Adding cardio past this point produces less deficit than the arithmetic suggests while spending real recovery. If fat loss stalls, the correct lever is the diet or the step count, not another cardio session.
Why the step floor is day-weighted rather than averaged. About 28% of exercise calories vanish to compensation (Careau) — NEAT quietly drifting down is how deficits stall while the training log still looks fine. Refined 31 Aug 2026. Flanagan et al. (2024, iScience) — a 24-week RCT using doubly-labelled water plus room calorimetry — found 48% of participants compensated, averaging −308 ± 158 kcal/day, with no significant metabolic adaptation in 24-hour, sleep or resting expenditure. The compensation is therefore behavioural: it happens in free-living movement, not in metabolism. That is why the floor is now day-weighted rather than averaged — an average is easily met by inflation on the days that already carry a cardio session, while the ones that do not quietly fall away. Note also that the constrained-expenditure model Careau represents is itself now contested: Howard et al. (2025, PNAS) found a positive linear relationship between physical activity and total expenditure, supporting an additive model. The disagreement does not change this prescription — both readings make the step floor the thing to defend.
The two mobility items that survived — moved here from the old cardio day on 17 Sep 2026. Couch stretch, 2 × 60 s per side, glute squeezed on the stretching side to bias the hip flexor: hip flexor length bears directly on lumbar position. Ankle dorsiflexion, 2 × 10 per side, knee over the second toe, heel down: two minutes, kept because it is cheap and running is the week’s only impact loading. Do both on a non-lifting day or in the evening — never in the first waking hour and never before a morning lift, where held stretching is contraindicated. The wider pre-training mobility circuit stays cut: Warneke (2024, 38 studies) found it no better than any other warm-up activity.

A protocol that is not followed has no effect size. This section is the one place where the best-evidenced option and the most-followed option genuinely diverge, and where the document deliberately picks the second.

Can this protocol detect its own effect?

⚠️ On the primary outcome, honestly: no — and no consumer measurement will change that. Judge the block on: the weight trend hitting −0.2 to −0.3 kg/week, the waist falling, and the logbook continuing to move. Those three are real instruments. A lean-mass readout is not.

12 — Evidence appendix

Every choice, its source, and how much to trust it

Confidence is graded on four things: study design, whether subjects were trained, whether independent sources agree, and — added 31 Aug 2026 — whether the effect is large enough for this field to detect at all. That last one deserves a number. Pooling 3,874 effect sizes across 417 studies, Swinton et al. (2022, J Sports Sci) derived strength-and-conditioning-specific thresholds: a medium effect is ≈0.29 SMD, far below Cohen’s generic 0.5. Combined with the near-universal sample sizes of 14–39 and the limits of B-mode ultrasound, the honest floor for a reliably detectable hypertrophy effect is roughly SMD 0.3–0.4, in a trial of at least 30–40 participants lasting 8–10 weeks. No sub-0.3 effect from a small trial should carry a High grade here, whatever its p-value. The rule that follows, and that this revision applied: adopt a small finding only when it lets you spend less time or less fatigue for the same result — never when it asks you to spend more for a claimed gain. High means multiple meta-analyses or trained-subject RCTs converge. Medium means the evidence is real but thin, mixed, or drawn from untrained subjects. Low means it is reasoned convention, a preprint, or a single underpowered study — flagged so you know which parts of this plan to abandon first if better evidence arrives.

⚠️ The structural caveat, added 31 Aug 2026 — read this before any individual row above. A count of meta-analyses in the table above overstates how independent this evidence base actually is. One laboratory or its immediate collaborators co-author the load-bearing citations for load, frequency, range of motion, lateral-raise implement, deload, and the volume dose–response. A second overlapping cluster produced essentially the entire proximity-to-failure literature. A third produced the partial-range studies and frequently co-publishes with the first. Even the statistical scrutiny is shared — the same methodologist appears across several of these groups’ papers. Disclosed competing interests recur: advisory-board seats at fitness-equipment manufacturers, a training-company cofounder, several authors earning income as industry writers, and all five authors of the single most load-bearing dose–response citation describing themselves as “coaches and writers in the fitness industry.” None of this is disqualifying, and none of it was hidden — every one of those disclosures is printed in the papers themselves. But it means this is roughly three or four densely cross-published groups rather than a field-wide consensus, and a finding that is overstated in that network propagates across many nominally separate papers because the same people and the same primary datasets recur. Where a claim in this document is carried by a genuinely independent group — Martikainen (Jyväskylä) on proximity to failure, Moreno (USF) on volume maintenance, Márquez (Granada) on paired sets, Arntz (no Warneke) on stretching — that is now said explicitly, because it is the strongest form of evidence available here.
Three citations carry explicit caveats and should not be load-bearing on their own. Enes (2025) was read as a preprint — the published version was not accessible, and it is the primary justification for the short warm-up. Oliva (2025, Eur J Transl Myol, doi 10.4081/ejtm.2025.14653), cited once in the warm-up section, is a self-described pilot of n = 8 whose between-warm-up difference was not significant (p = 0.186); it is not evidence for or against the warm-up used here. The time-crunched variant — same five days, about 50–60 minutes each

Built by cutting sets, never by cutting exercises or shortening rests. Every muscle keeps both exposures; the week drops from 99 sets to 78 (Day 1: 14, Day 2: 18, Day 3: 14, Day 4: 15, Day 5: 17). Against the ACSM’s ≥10 sets/wk higher-volume comparison group (a comparison group, not a floor), back (12 direct), chest (11) and glute max (10) stay at or above it; quads (9) and hamstrings (7) dip below it, and side delts fall to 6 direct (8 effective) and calves to 5. That is a deliberate short-term compromise for weeks when time is the constraint, not a standing plan.

Corrected 15 Oct 2026 — about 50–60 minutes a session, not ~40. This heading previously said “~40 minutes each”. Run through the same session-time model as the totals above (the model is spelled out in the four-day variant section), with the rests unchanged as the rules below require and one ramp set kept per exercise, the cut sessions come to ~49 · ~57 · ~49 · ~57 · ~62 = 274 minutes of lifting, against the full plan’s 328. Add the 6–9 minute warm-up to each. The saving is real but uneven — about 11, 4, 9, 26 and 4 minutes a session — because the rests, ramps and station changes do not shrink when sets are cut. Treat these as targets, not standards, as with every other time on this page.

  • Heavy tier drops from 4 sets to 3. Applies to the Day 1 pulldown, Day 3 chest press and Day 4 hack squat. Rest stays at 150 s — this is the one place not to economise.
  • Every light-tier isolation drops to 2 sets. The last set was already the one taken to failure, so the failure set survives. Light tier here means the failure-set lifts, including the standing calf raise. That takes the Day 2 Seated Leg Curl from 4 sets to 2. Two slots stay at 3: the seated calf raise, because it is not taken to failure and so is not light-tier; and the two Captain’s Chair Knee Raise slots, which do go to failure on the last set but are kept at 3 as a deliberate exception, because abs are already only 6 sets a week and cutting them further would save about two minutes a session.
  • Take the Day 4 Smith lunge to 2 sets and hip abduction to 1. Glute-max volume falls from 12 direct sets to 10 (hip thrust 5 + RDL 3 + lunge 2, under the single counting rule) and gluteus medius from 3 to 1, which is the largest proportional cut in the short version.
  • Do not cut rest below 60 s. In the one within-subject trial that isolated this (Longo 2022, n=28), reps collapsed at 60 s and quadriceps growth halved — 13.1% at 180 s versus 6.8% at 60 s — with the gap disappearing only when volume load was artificially restored. The cost is not the clock; it is the reps. Supersets were also banned here and no longer are — Zhang et al. (2025) found no hypertrophy cost (SMD = −0.05, p = 0.87). They are banned here for a different and simpler reason: the trainee does not superset, in any plan — he trains alone at 05:30 and will not hold two stations. They would also be the wrong tool for this variant regardless, whose premise is doing less work rather than compressing the same work. If what you want is the full week’s sets in less weekly time, that is the four-day variant, which buys it by dropping a session rather than by pairing — and pays for it with two upper sessions of ~92 and ~91 minutes.

What to drop first, in order

This ordering is the same one used by the weekly landmine in Progression, extended down to the point where you should stop and reconsider the deficit instead.

  1. Day 4 Smith lunge, down to 2 sets — highest bracing tax in the session. Do not zero it; it is one of only two glute-max exposures in the week.
  2. Day 4 hip abduction, down to 2 sets — cheap, but do not zero it: it is one of the two glute exposures.
  3. Third set of any light-tier isolation — take them to two, starting with the exercises whose muscle has three weekly exposures.
  4. Day 1 face pull, down to 2 sets — rear delts still receive substantial fractional volume from 13 pulling sets.
  5. Any interval session you run — the highest injury-risk item in the week when you are under-fuelled.
  6. Your standalone cardio session — but keep the step floor. The steps are worth more than the walk.
  7. Stop here and raise calories instead. If you are still under-recovered after all six, the problem is the deficit, not the program — and Murphy & Koehler says the deficit is the variable that was supposed to stay small.
⚠️ You train alone, so three lifts have a modified failure rule. The failure rule elsewhere in this document — final set of light-tier single-joint work goes to momentary failure — assumes you can fail a rep safely. Three lifts do not qualify, and none of them was a failure lift anyway; this makes it explicit rather than implied.

Flat dumbbell bench press (Day 5) — stop at 1–2 RIR, always. A failed rep here means dropping dumbbells from a supine position with no one to take them. If you cannot set the dumbbells down under control at the end of a set, the load is too heavy regardless of how the reps felt. Incline dumbbell press (Day 3) — same rule, same reason. Romanian deadlift (Day 2) — already capped at 2–3 RIR and flagged never-to-zero; training alone is a second reason, not a new one.

Everything taken to failure in this plan is a machine or a cable: pec deck, lateral raises, pushdowns, curls, leg extension, leg curl, face pull, standing calf raise. All of them can be failed safely with the weight going nowhere dangerous. That is not a coincidence — it is why the failure sets were put where they were.
What is explicitly not in this plan, and will not be added. No second chest press, no third row variation, no barbell back squat or conventional deadlift, no unsupported bent-over rowing, no cable kickbacks, no B-stance hip thrust, no second rear-delt day, no behind-the-neck pressing or pulling, no static stretching before lifting, no foam rolling, no pre-workout mobility circuit, no post-activation potentiation protocol (Xu 2025, Sports Medicine, 62 studies: effect size 0.12, shrinking as training status rises, and not one measured outcome was hypertrophy), and no fourth ramping set on anything. Each of these was considered and rejected on the evidence, not on preference.
